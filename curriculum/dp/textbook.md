Inclusive design seeks to break down barriers and ensure
that experiences are accessible and usable by everyone,
regardless of physical or cognitive differences. Inclusive
design represents an extension of User-Centred Design
(UCD) in that it aims to include those members of the
community who might otherwise be forgotten during the
product design process, such as those who are pregnant,
elderly, disabled, or injured and through design attempts
to improve the lives of all people.
Inclusive design principles (sometimes called ‘Universal
Design, ‘Universal Usability Design, ‘Accessible Design’
and ‘Design for All') are increasingly being incorporated
into building codes. The term Universal Design was
originally used by Ron Mace, (1998) and was coined to
meet the needs of people of all ages and abilities.
The Centre for Universal Design defines the term as, ..
the design of products and environment to be usable by all
people, to the greatest extent possible, without the need for
adaptation or specialised design. The intent of universal
design is to simplify life for everyone by making products, communications and the built environment more usable
by as many people as possible at little or no cost.
Centre for Universal Design, 2005
Universal design takes into consideration people of all
ages, genders and abilities and through design attempts
to improve the lives of all people. In the past, it has been
misconstrued as ‘one size fits all' This is less than its full
intention. Through legislation and public education
campaigns, designers have come to realise their social
responsibilities. They have recognised that good design
reflects the needs of a variety of users and makes
accommodations for this range.
The main features of Universal Design may be interpreted
as:
+ equity in use
« flexibility in service
« allowing tolerance for error
« simple and intuitive operation
« communicate unambiguous information
« need low physical effort to function
« provide space for operators and use
In many industrialised nations, legislation exists covering
and enforcing consideration of people with a disability.
Human factors research drives and informs designs based
on these legislative requirements.
Just some of the countries with legislation requiring
consideration of individuals with a disability include:
Australia, the Disability Discrimination Act 1992
« (Canada, the Ontarians with Disabilities Act of 2001
« US, under the Americans with Disabilities Act of
1990
« South Africa, the Promotion of Equality and Prevention
of Unfair Discrimination Act 2000
« India, Persons with Disabilities (Equal Opportunities,
Protection of Rights and Full Participation) Act,
1995.
In developing countries, formal legislation may not be
enacted, but through a series of informal guidelines
and international standards promoted by NGOs and
government agencies, disability issues may be addressed
to some degree.
Instances of everyday items where inclusive design can
be found are in the design of currency, where coins of
varying size, thickness and edge detail allow those with
poor sight to avoid confusion. Similarly, many countries
have adopted notes with differing sizes, colours and
textural details that assist the vision impaired.

Note: As introduced in Section A 3.2.1, all structures,
such as bridges, are designed and built considering
the same fundamental forces, e.g. tensile, compressive,
shear, bending and torsional. These structural forces are
categorised as either static or dynamic.
Static vs. Dynamic Forces
Static forces are constant and unchanging over time. Also
known as deadloads, they include forces derived from the
mass of a structure. They maybe long or short term. They
may change slowly but involve no impact or vibration.
Examples include the force of a building’s roof pressing
down on the walls, furniture such as desks, bookshelves
and beds exerting a constant force on the floor or a parked
car resting on a bridge or a garage floor, etc.
Dynamic or live forces are changing or moving forces,
including shock, impact, or vibration (e.g. periodic,
random, transient. Examples include loads from vehicular
and pedestrian traffic, vehicle braking, expansion and
contraction, wind, water from stream flow and ground
movements, etc.
Compression
Forces applied to an object that try to squash or reduce
the object in size are identified as compressive forces.
Examples include forces on columns, arches and load-
bearing walls. The legs of a chair in use are pushed down
by your weight, placing the chair legs into a state of
compression.
Tension
Tensile forces are those applied to a body that attempt
to stretch or make the body longer. Therefore, tension
is a pulling force that stretches or elongates a material.
Examples include: suspension cables, ropes, tie rods or a
rope in a tug-of-war battle that is being pulled from both
ends.
Torsion
A twisting force that rotates a structure or object around
its axis. Acts on: Drive shafts, door handles, and structural
beams under torque. Examples include twisting a
screwdriver or wringing out a wet towel.
Bending
Bending forces cause a material to curve, i.e. a combination
of tension and compression on opposite sides. A wooden
ruler, when pushed down at one end, demonstrates the
principle of bending. Examples include bridges, beams
and diving boards,
Shear
A force that slides layers of material against each other in
opposite directions is known as a shear force. Shear stresses
are generated in a material by the action of these forces.
Shear stresses may be single, double or punching stress.
Examples of shear forces include lateral forces on bolts
and the act of trimming paper or fabric with scissors.
Tensile, compressive and shear forces are most often
measured in megapascals.

Stress (o) is the relationship between an applied load and
a material’s cross-sectional area, measured in pascals (Pa).
Pascals are a unit of pressure and reflect a force of one
Newton per square metre or N/; 'm?. In some calculations,
cross-sectional area is determined in mm’, giving stress in
terms of N/mm? (N mm™) or megapascals.
Strain (g) is an engineering quantity that measures the
change in length of a body relative to its original length
when subjected to a load. Strain is a dimensionless
quantity.
Young’s Modulus is the ratio of stress to strain within a
material’s elastic region.
It quantifies a material’s stiffness or how resistant it is to
elastic deformation. Young’s Modulus is shown graphically
as the slope of the straight-line portion at the beginning
of a stress-strain graph. As an example, steel has a high
Young’s Modulus (very stiff) while rubber has a very low
one.
Yield Strength is defined as the stress at which a material
begins to deform plastically. It indicates the transition from
reversible (elastic) to irreversible (plastic) deformation.
On a stress-strain graph, it is the point where the curve
starts to bend away from the straight line. After this
point, the material won't return to its original shape when
unloaded.
Ultimate Strength, or tensile strength, is the maximum
stress the material can withstand. It is also the peak load-
bearing capacity before necking begins. On a stress-strain
graph, it is the highest point on the stress-strain curve.
Beyond this point, the material starts to weaken and
deform rapidly.
'The fracture point indicates total failure of the material. It
is the last reading on the stress-strain graph.

A Safety Factor of 1 means that the structure is designed to
withstand exactly the maximum expected load, no more,
no less.
In practical terms, this means that if the factor of
safety is one, then the strength value of the structure is
equivalent to the maximum allowed load. As such, there
is no margin for error, unexpected stress, material flaws or
environmental changes.
Example:
If a beam is expected to carry 1500 N, and it's designed to
fail at 1500 N, then:
« SF=1500N+1000N=1
Designing with a factor of safety of one is risky because it
assumes:
« Perfect material quality
+ No unexpected loads (e.g, wind, earthquakes,
misuse)
« No degradation over time (e.g., corrosion, fatigue)
This is rarely realistic in the real world.
Reasons why engineers build in extra strength to account
for uncertainties include:
« Material variability: real-world materials may have
flaws or inconsistencies.
« Load unpredictability: actual loads may exceed
estimates due to accidents or environmental factors,
« Wear and ageing: structures degrade over time due to
fatigue, corrosion or weather.
« Human safety: lives may depend on the structure’s
integrity—failure is unacceptable.

ability to modify motion by changing its speed, direction
or power of motion. This is achieved through various
mechanisms like gears, belt drives, cams, pulleys, levers

Gears can increase or decrease rotational speed. A larger

Belt Drives are mechanical systems that transmit power

Cams are mechanical components primarily used to
transform rotary motion into linear motion, or to create
a specific pattern of motion in a contacting element
called a follower. They are essential in various machines
and mechanisms, including engines, machine tools and
sewing machines.
Pulleys, particularly in combination, can increase or
decrease speed by changing the circumference of the
pulleys used to transmit motion.
Levers can amplify the force applied to an object and also
redirect forces, making it easier to lift or move things.

An analogue signal is one in which energy is continuously
varying, such as a sine wave. The value of the energy in
the analogue wave can be at any particular value, within
limits, at any instant.
The process of modifying an analogue signal to ensure
that the signal is strong, clean and within the acceptable
range for the next stage of processing or measurement is
known as signal conditioning and is performed with the
aid of Op-amps.
A number of processes are undertaken during signal
conditioning, including amplification, attenuation,
filtering, isolation, AC coupling, DC offset correction and
event detection to prepare the signal for accurate analysis
or digital conversion.
« Amplification: Increasing the signal's amplitude to
make it stronger.
« Attenuation: decreases the signal's amplitude to make
it weaker.
« Filtering: Removing unwanted noise or interference by
selectively passing or blocking certain frequencies
« Isolation: Protecting the signal from electrical noise or
ground loops.
« AC Coupling: Blocking DC components while
allowing AC signals to pass through. This is often
achieved by placing a capacitor in the circuit, which
acts as a high-pass filter for AC signals and a low-pass
filter for DC signals
« DC Offset: Shifting the signal’s DC level to a desired
value, This ensures that the output signal has the
correct DC level, even if there are DC errors in the
input signal
« Event Detection: Identifying specific events or changes
in the signal.
Digital
Rather than being a continuously varying wave, a digital
signal is comprised of a sequence of discrete pulses in
which energy is either ‘on’ or ‘off” and can therefore be
represented by the digits 1 and 0. Each of these is termed a
‘bit’. This is described as a binary signal in which the signal
has only one of two possible values.
Digital program control systems use a series of instructions

within a digital circuit. This allows for complex logic and
decision-making.
These systems rely on several key electronic components
such as logic gates, microcontrollers/microprocessors,
digital sensors, analogue-to-digital converters (ADCs),
digital-to-analogue converters (DACs) and digital signal
processors (DSPs).

to transfer the code. This process is often facilitated by
software, such as Microchip’s MPLABX IDE, that compiles
the code and then uploads it to the microcontroller.
Input pins on a PIC microcontroller are designed to
recognise different voltage levels. Generally, they will treat
voltages above a certain threshold as a digital '1' (or logic
high), and voltages below that threshold as a digital '0'
(or logic low). Input pins are often connected to external
devices like switches, sensors, or other logic circuits. The
voltage level at the input pin will then reflect the state of that
external device. Similarly, output pins can be configured
to output a specific voltage level. By writing a '1' or '0' to
a particular output port, the microcontroller can set that
pin’s voltage to its high or low state, respectively.
Some PIC microcontrollers can also read analogue
voltage levels. This is typically done using an Analogue-
to-Digital Converter (ADC). The ADC will convert the
analogue voltage into a digital representation that the
microcontroller can process.
Because the PIC is capable of storing and running a simple
program, many modern electronic devices incorporate
PICs to provide various programmable options. The
program options available in modern washing machines,
refrigerators, microwave ovens, smartphones and
televisions are provided through PICs.
The flexibility of the PIC comes from the wide range of
functions for which it can be utilised. While the capacity
is largely unnecessary in any one application where a
PIC is suitable, the use ofa purpose-built controller for
every application, to ensure that full use of capability is
made, would be time consuming and expensive to build
and maintain compared with the convenience of a single
component that can satisfy a large number of different
requirements albeit with redundant capabilities.
The inclusion of PICs in a product can therefore allow the
reduction of other circuitry, and because PICs can replace
many circuit components, stock levels can be reduced
and assembly simplified. This reduction of components
and simplification of assembly can lead to lower cost and
greater product reliability.
The flexibility of the PICs, therefore, provides benefits like
high performance, low cost, greater reliability, simplified
assembly and maintenance and reduced inventory needs
due to interchangeability and programmability. The ability
to program PICs also gives them an extended life.

pen- and closed-loop electronic circuits are used to
control the dynamic behaviour or amplification factor of a
system, known as the ‘gain’
In an open-loop system, an input is accepted and operated
on before an output is created, as illustrated in Figure
A 3.4.11a. An open loop does not monitor the output to
modify the input signal, meaning there is no feedback
loop. The gain is therefore solely dependent on the internal
characteristics of the amplifier.
Because of this, any changes in the processing of the input
that might affect the output are not taken into account.
An example of an open loop would be the operation of a
simple light switch in which the light is turned on or off
without any feedback to adjust the brightness.
In a closed-loop system, the output is fed back and
compared with the desired result. This information is then
used to adjust the input to modify the output, as illustrated
by the block diagram for a closed-loop system in Figure
A34.11b.
Examples of closed-loop systems would be the operation
of a thermostat to maintain a desired temperature,
automobile cruise control and automatic brightness
control of PCs and phones.
There are two basic forms of feedback encountered
in electronic systems, known as positive and negative
feedbacks. Both forms of feedback return part of the
output signal to a comparator circuit, which compares the
output produced with the desired output. If the signals
differ, an error signal is produced that is used to adjust
the input signal. Then the cycle continues until the desired
output requirements are achieved.
Positive feedback systems do this by adding the return
signal to the input signal, and can result in a rapid cycle of
amplification (as illustrated in Figure A3.4.11c), leading
to system overload or saturation, meaning that the output
voltage is as positive as it is capable of being.
The annoying squeal sometimes encountered with a
microphone is an example of positive feedback. Positive
feedback is used in systems where rapid transition between
positive and negative saturation is desired.
When the input voltage is higher or lower than the
reference voltage, the output voltage moves to either
the positive or negative saturation positions, such that a
continuously variable input is transformed into a square
wave that undergoes abrupt step changes of positive to
negative, shown in Figure A3.4.11d. This is sometimes
referred to as clipping.
By contrast, in negative feedback systems, the return signal
is subtracted from the input, resulting in a reduction of
the input and a tendency to form a self-correcting loop
that will drive the system to stabilisation at the aim. Such
systems can be prone to ‘hunting, however, (in which the
output overshoots the aim and oscillates towards its goal),
if an excessive delay in the feedback signal is present, as
illustrated in Figure A3.4.11d
Negative feedback loops can also be prone to saturation
if the system reaches a physical or operational limit, such
as a maximum capacity or a point where further input
doesn’t produce a change in output. In these cases, the
negative feedback mechanism is still operating, but its
effect is limited by the constraints of the system.
While both systems have their advantages, most use
negative rather than positive feedback.

Guiding question
Why is material selection crucial to designers of
products?
Material selection is crucial in product design because it
directly impacts a product’s functionality, aesthetics, cost,
manufacturability and environmental impact. Choosing
the right materials is essential for ensuring a product
performs as intended, meets design requirements and is
commercially viable.

B3.1.1
The initial step in selecting materials for a specific
application and design involves clearly defining the
necessary properties the product must have, through
an understanding of the products intended use and
the specific conditions it will encounter. This includes
considering factors like strength, stiffness, resistance to
environmental degradation, extremes of heat and cold,
impact resistance, abrasion resistance, etc.
Based on the performance requirements, a selection of
potential materials that best meet the properties and
performance criteria is developed and often involves the
use of material selection charts (Ashby Charts) to evaluate
different materials.
The key material properties that will ensure the product
functions as intended need to be identified. These
properties might include tensile strength, toughness,
flexibility, thermal conductivity, corrosion resistance, UV
tolerance, etc., strength, durability, cost and environmental
impact, as well as how the material will be processed which
often requires compromises.
The choice of material will also be influenced by the
manufacturing process, as some materials are easier
to machine, mould or weld than others, in addition
to consideration of the cost and feasibility of different
manufacturing methods.
The environmental impact of the material throughout its
lifecycle (from production to disposal) is also becoming
increasingly important and needs to be factored into the
material selection process.
Material selection charts
(Ashby Charts)
Material selection charts, also known as Ashby plots, are
graphical tools used in materials science and engineering
to visualise and compare the properties of different
materials. Developed in the 1990s by Professor Michael
Ashby of Cambridge University, Ashby plots are an
invaluable tool for material selection in various design
applications.
These charts typically plot material properties on a
logarithmic scale, allowing for comparison of different
materials against selected performance indices such as
strength versus density. By plotting performance indices
for a variety of materials on the same graph, those materials
best suited for specific applications are highlighted.
Ashby plots also reveal trade-offs between different
properties. For instance, a material might have high
strength but also high density, which can be easily seen
on the plot. By drawing lines of constant performance
(often called ‘design lines’), engineers can quickly screen
materials that satisfy the desired performance criteria and then further refine their selection based on other factors.
The plot of strength versus maximum service temperature
narrows down candidate materials for an application requiring high-temperature strength, while revealing the limitations of other material classes such as polymers, under such conditions.
With the aid of such charts, an initial screening 
and selection can be undertaken by identifying materials
that meet specific performance requirements, such as
mechanical properties, environmental impact, corrosion
resistance, manufacturability, density or cost.
Ashby plots allow:
« the retrieval of the typical properties of a particular
material,
« a comparison of the properties of different materials,
revealing their comparative efficiencies,
« the selection of the materials/manufacturing processes
during the product design stage, and
«+ substitution studies exploring the potential of one
material to replace another.
Performance Indices
Beyond basic property comparisons, Ashby plots can
be used to visualise performance indices, which are
combinations of material properties that are relevant to a
specific application. These indices are typically commonly
applied mathematical relationships such as specific
strength (o, / p), specific stiffness (E / p) and fracture
toughness (MPa.m) but can include any relevant measure
such as the ratio of any property with respect to cost e.g.
the ratio of Thermal Conductivity to cost (A / §) or energy
per cubic metre as illustrated in Figure B 3.1.1c.
By combining material properties into a single value,
performance indices enable users to quickly rank
materials and identify the most suitable options for a
given design or function. Using an Ashby plot of strength
versus specific stiffness, for example, identifies materials
that are strong and stiff for their weight. Such a plot would
also reveal trade-offs, such as materials that might be
very strong but also heavy, while others might be stiff but
relatively weak. The Ashby plot shown in Figure B 3.1.1c
of strength against energy per cubic metre shows the
relationship between a material’s ability to withstand force
and its energy content per unit volume. It helps identify
materials that offer the best strength for a given energy
input, guiding material selection for applications where
energy efficiency is crucial.
Materials with higher strength-to-energy ratios will be
located towards the upper left corner of the plot. This
indicates that they can withstand greater forces for a given
amount of energy input. This information is valuable
for selecting materials for lightweight structures, energy
storage devices or other applications where minimising
energy consumption is important.

Life-cycle assessment (LCA) is an internationally
standardised process to assess environmental impacts
on products and services. It is a tool to support decision-
making for manufacturers when assessing the impact a
product or process has on the environment. Specifically,
the process involves all stages of product development,

LCA is a relatively young technique; it became popular in
the early 1990s. Initially, many thought that LCA would
be a suitable tool to support environmental claims that
could directly be used in marketing. Over the years, it has
become clear that this is not the best application for LCA,
although it is important to communicate LCA results in a
careful and well-balanced way.
In recent years, life-cycle thinking (LCT) has become a key

communicated by the EU. Also in Asia and the Americas,
many countries have developed strategies that promote
life-cycle thinking as a key concept.
Another development is the sustainability reporting
movement. “The majority of the Fortune 500 companies
now report on the sustainability aspects of their
operations.” (PRé Consultants, Introduction to LCA with
SimaPro, 2008).
Life-cycle analysis is just one way companies are becoming
more responsible for manufacturing-based emissions and
retrieving product waste. It also forces designers to think
about changing product design to favour the environment,
i.e. developing sustainable product designs that consider
energy, materials efficiencies, manufacturing processes,

Paper-based rapid prototyping, involves the use of

Selective deposition lamination (SDL) was invented in

On completion the surrounding waste portions of paper

Rapid prototyping using CAD modelling and 3D printing
technology to rapidly create a design and manufacture an
object allows initial testing and design validation to be
undertaken. The low cost and rapidity with which such
models can be created allows for multiple prototypes to
be printed as part of an iterative approach to product

By contrast, traditional prototyping techniques have

planning and a longer development cycle, although
offering improved surface finish and finer detail compared

While rapid 3D prototyping is used to quickly create

production techniques typically prioritise precision,
material properties and cost-effectiveness for large-scale
manufacturing, potentially shifting to methods like
injection moulding or CNC machining.

rolling. The mould is then allowed to cool before being

steel into a mould in a continuous process to create a long

the mid-1950s and today accounts for over 96% of global

In continuous casting, molten steel is poured from a ladle
into an intermediate container known as a tundish. The
tundish serves as the place where slag and impurities are
removed from the melt and from there it enters an open
ended water-cooled reciprocating copper mould.
As long as the tundish is supplied with sufficient metal
to continue casting into the mould, casting can continue
uninterrupted. Therefore as the main ladle is emptied a
new ladle is usually waiting to take its place to continue a
sequence.
In modern steelmaking the continuous casting process
may also be integrated with metal rolling. This secondary
rolling operation is a forming process that changes the
shape of the metal it processes.
Continuous casting is very economical in that the rolling
mill can be fed directly from the continuously cast metal
casting strand with the rate of the rolling operation
carefully synchronised with the speed of the continuous
metal casting, effectively combining the two operations as
one.

Pressure die-casting (also known as injection die-casting):
involves the rapid injection of non-ferrous metals under
high pressure into a closed die cavity and is particularly
suited to high volume production.
Die-casting has an enormous range of applications, with
many manufacturing industries relying on one of the
many variations of pressure die-casting that include hot-
chamber and cold-chamber die-casting.
Hot chamber machines utilise a curved conduit known
as a ‘gooseneck’ that connects a molten metal holding pot
to the injection system, as shown in Figure A 4.1.8f. As
the casting process begins a piston raises a plunger at the
bottom of the gooseneck creating a vacuum and drawing
molten metal into the gooseneck, filling it. The piston
then rapidly lowers the plunger forcing the molten metal
under pressure from the gooseneck into the die where it
solidifies.
In order to avoid damage to the gooseneck and injection
system, this method is generally confined to use with
the lower melting point metals such as tin (232°C), lead
(328°C) and zinc (420°C) and some of the lower melting
point Mg-Al system alloys such as AZ91D and AM50.
Cold chamber machines are particularly useful for the
casting of non-ferrous metals with a melting point too
high for the hot chamber process, such as magnesium
(650°C), aluminium (660°C) and copper (1085°C). This
process utilises a separate furnace where the metal is
melted and held at the appropriate casting temperature, as
shown in Figure A 4.1.8g.
In the cold chamber die casting process, molten metal is
transferred from a furnace into a ‘shot’ chamber sleeve. A
plunger within this sleeve is raised to create a vacuum that
syphons a measured amount of molten metal from the
furnace into the chamber. Subsequently, the molten metal
is injected at high pressure into the die.
Pressure is maintained until the casting has solidified and
cooled sufficiently so that the mould can be opened and
the cast part ejected using injection pins. The die is then
closed ready for the next cycle.
In order to avoid problems of trapped air within the die
that might adversely affect the quality of the casting two
techniques for air removal are used. These involve either
the use of a vacuum to evacuate air prior to metal injection,
or the provision of several small channels leading from the
die cavity to the outside known as vents. The vents allow
the displacement of air from the die as the metal enters.
Unlike other casting processes die-casting does not
incorporate a metal reservoir such as a riser to feed
the cooling and contracting metal. Metal shrinkage is
controlled by the injection pressure and appropriate
design of the metal flow.
Pressure die casting is used extensively for products such
as automotive trim, handles, iron sole plates, disc drive
chassis, musical instruments, rotors for electrical motors,
kitchen faucets and steering wheels.
Sand casting: involves the production of a temporary
mould produced containing a cavity of the shape desired.
As the name implies, these moulds are manufactured from
sand and are typically produced in a facility dedicated to
producing metal castings called a foundry.
The traditional and still most common method of
producing a sand mould is to pour a mixture of sand and
various binding agents into boxes containing a half pattern
of the casting. Following the setting of the moulding sand,
the pattern is removed and the cavity surface is painted
with a refractory slurry that acts as a barrier to prevent
scouring as the molten metal enters.
The final mould is subsequently constructed by joining
the individual parts, see Figure A4.1.8h. The top half of
the mould is known as the cope while the bottom half is
the drag. Intricate and hollow shapes are created by the
incorporation of appropriately formed internal shapes, also
made of sand, known as cores. Provision is made during
the moulding process for metal entry into the mould and
the feeding’ of the casting during solidification.
Today, 3D printing is also being used to produce moulds
using additive manufacturing techniques like binder
jetting (BJ) or selective laser sintering (SLS). These
methods create moulds by selectively binding sand
particles together, layer by layer, according to a digital 3D
model. This process allows for complex geometries and
intricate details that are difficult or impossible to achieve
with traditional methods.
Figure A 4.1.8i shows molten metal being cast from a
small ladle into the sprue of a sand mould. In this figure
a number of weights can be seen on top of the mould,
positioned to ensure that hydrostatic pressure does not
result in the cope lifting.
Castings solidify first at those positions where heat
removal is greatest. These positions generally correspond
with areas where the molten metal comes into contact with
the mould wall. Solidification therefore usually proceeds
from the outer surface toward the casting’s interior.
As the casting solidifies, it contracts and the molten centre
‘feeds’ the growing front of solidifying metal. If extra
molten metal is not supplied, the last part of the cavity
to solidify will have insufficient metal to be completely
solid and a ‘shrinkage’ cavity will result. To aveid this
situation reservoirs of molten metal are often provided
by the incorporation of ‘risers’ into the mould. Risers are
designed to be the last part of the casting to solidify and
act to feed molten metal into the casting.
‘The heat from the casting gradually degrades the binding
agent and the mould crumbles allowing easy removal of
the casting called ‘shakeout’ or ‘knock-out’ ready for the
removal of risers and gating systems known as fettling,
see Figure A 4.1.8j.
If correctly designed, the casting is free of shrinkage (it
is ‘'sound’) and any shrinkage is contained completely
within the risers, which are removed during the fettling’
or clean-up stage.
Because these castings can be relatively large the grain-
size can also be large leading to lower strength. Grain-
size is controlled by adjusting casting temperature and
solidification rate and with the addition of grain-refining
elements along with later heat treatments.
Remember, as a general rule, slow cooling of molten metals
produces larger grains than rapid cooling which produces
grains of a smaller size. Manipulation of grain size may
also take place after cooling through such heat treatment
operations as annealing, quenching or normalising.
Precision casting: also known as investment casting (IC)
and lost wax casting refers to a metal casting process where
a wax pattern is created, coated with a refractory material
to form a mould, and then the wax is melted out, leaving a
cavity into which molten metal is poured.
Lost wax casting, represents a subset of sand casting
and based on archaeological evidence, has its origins in
Mesopotamia around 3000-4000 BCE. It appears that
the process was in use in most civilisations of the ancient
world, mainly for artistic purposes, from jewellery to
statuary both large and small. The bronze statue of Perseus
holding the head of Medusa produced by Benvenuto
Cellini in 1554, and the aluminium statue of Eros in
London'’s Piccadilly Circus cast by Alfred Gilbert in 1893
(Figure A4.1.8k), are famous examples of the process.
While the lost wax process continued to be used in artistic
circles because of the intricate detail, excellent surface
finish and near net shape characteristics it offers, the
mainstream engineering use of the process did not begin
until the Second World War. Atthattime, capacity restraints
in machining led to the use of the lost wax process because
of its ability to produce compenents that did not require
machining. Following the war, the development of the jet
engine created an increasing demand for the process in
the production of high precision castings of refractory
metals used for components such as gas turbine blades.
The lost wax casting process shown in Figure A 4.1.8],
has traditionally used a permanent mould or die often
made of metal to produce an expendable wax pattern.
The pattern may then be incorporated into an assembly
with similar patterns and attached to a wax feeding
system. The whole assembly is subsequently dipped into
a ceramic slurry to build up a coating. Fine sand is then
added to form a strong shell and the assembly allowed to
dry. The dry assembly is then placed in a mould flask and
surrounded by a coarser sand/slurry mixture and dried.
This coarser sand/slurry backing is technically known as
the investment. After setting, the mould is gently heated
to melt the wax pattern leaving a smooth mould cavity
behind. In the final stage of production, metal is cast into
the mould to form the part.
Controlled breakdown of the sand/binder mixture from
exposure to the temperature of the solidifying metal
allows the solidified casting to be easily removed from
the mould for fettling. During the fettling operation the
casting is separated from the metal feeder system of sprue,
runners and in-gates.

Pultrusion: is similar to extrusion with the exception that

Compression Moulding: represents a generic term for

Injection moulding is a manufacturing process where
molten thermoplastic is injected under pressure into
a closed mould, as illustrated in Figure A4.1.8r. The
process involves three main parts: the injection unit, the
mould and the clamping unit. Plastic pellets are fed into
the injection unit via a hopper and melted by a heating
unit. The molten plastic is then injected into the mould,
where it cools and solidifies. Finally, the finished product
is ejected from the mould after the clamps are released.
The injection unit consists of the polymer processing
section where polymer granules are heated to a molten
state ready for injection, while the clamping unit
comprises a permanent mould consisting of two platens,
one of which is fixed and the other moveable.
Products are manufactured one after another in an
automated cycle in which polymer granules loaded into
the feed hopper are fed under gravity into the barrel
containing a screw system. Heaters on the outer surface
of the barrel melt the polymer as it is carried by the screw
towards the end, where a non-return valve allows molten
material to build-up increasing hydraulic back-pressure
against the screw. Sensors monitor this pressure and when
sufficient molten material has accumulated, rotation of the
screw is stopped and the hydraulic cylinder and ram move
the screw forward and the accumulated molten polymer
is pushed through the nozzle and into the sprue, injecting
the polymer into the die. The molten polymer passes along
a runner system machined into the platen surface before
passing into the product. The runner system is therefore
located at the die parting line.
The mould remains closed until the polymer has cooled
sufficiently, following which the movable platen is
retracted, and the part formed is ejected. Although the
initial capital cost of machinery and dies is high, surface
finishes are excellent, intricate detail can be achieved and
large production runs lower costs per unit.
The process is similar to injection die casting of metals,
although in the case of injection moulding the material being injected under high pressure is molten plastic (both
thermosets and thermoplastics).

Permanent joining techniques are those that, once
formed, cannot easily be separated without damage.
Because of this, such items are not easily repaired or
modified, tending instead to be discarded in favour of a
new item exhibiting the functions and capacities desired.
Permanent joining techniques include the use of adhesives,

generally considered to be attained through the interaction

typically involves the introduction of a thin layer of liquid
polymer (the adhesive) between the surfaces to be joined.
The adhesive undergoes polymerisation in-situ.
Adhesives, depending on their bonding action, may allow
for the separation of parts with little or no damage to the
original surfaces. They are commonly used to join thin

cause distortion or damage. Some adhesives, composed

componentry.

Soldering and brazing: are similar operations and involve
the joining of two or more parts by melting a filler metal
with a heat source. The molten filler metal flows into the
joint and on solidifying, holds the parts together. The heat
source for soldering is usually a hot ‘iron’ while brazing is
accomplished with the aid of an oxyacetylene torch orasa
bulk process in a furnace.
The primary difference between the soldering and brazing
processes is the temperature at which the filler metal (often
an alloy) solidifies. The dividing line between the processes
is somewhat unclear however, as some definitions quote
a liquidus temperature (where the alloy is completely
liquid) of approximately 450°C, while others a solidus
(where the alloy is completely solid) of 500°C. For metal
alloys there is generally a mushy zone in which the metal
is in a partially liquid/solid state, while for pure metals the
solidus and liquidus are the same temperature.
Perhaps because of this, terminology is sometimes
confusing, as with the process of ‘silver soldering, which
usesasilver alloy filler metal with a liquidus of about 450°C
and which is considered to be a brazing operation.
Regardless of the exact division, soldering, shown in
Figure A 4.1.9¢, uses filler metals with a solidification
temperatures <500°C (e.g. Pb, Sn, Sb etc.) while brazing
generally uses alloys with solidification temperatures
>500°C (e.g. Cu, Ni etc.) alloys
A section through a brazed joint between a copper
tube and copper expansion bellows performed by silver
soldering is shown in Figure A 4.1.9d.
The primary requirement of both processes is that the
surfaces to be joined are free of oxides, so that the filler
metal can ‘wet’ the surface. Fluxes designed to remove
oxides from the surface are used to provide a clean joint.
Both brazing and soldering rely on forming a metallurgical
bond through the dissolution of a small amount of the
base metal at the interface, resulting in the formation of
intermetallic compounds. This is achieved by heating the
joint to a temperature below the melting point of the base
metals but high enough to melt the filler metal. The filler
metal then flows into the joint via capillary action, creating
a strong bond through the metallurgical interaction with
the base metal.
Surface Mounting Technology (SMT): is a specialised
soldering technology and the dominant method for
assembling electronic components onto Printed Circuit
Boards (PCBs) used today. SMT replaces the older
technology where component leads were passed through
drilled holes in the board and soldered to pads on the
opposite side.
Using SMT, solder paste consisting of a mixture of solder
and flux, is precisely applied to designated pads on the PCB,
where the surface mount components will be positioned.
Components are placed directly onto the board’s surface
following which the PCB is passed through a reflow oven.
The heat in the oven melts the solder paste, creating a
strong electrical and mechanical connection between
the component leads and the PCB pads. The assembly
is then cooled, solidifying the solder and completing the
connection, see Figure A 4.1.9.
The use of surface mounting technology has provided
improvements over the older ‘through hole’ method by
allowing higher component density, smaller and lighter
components, faster automated assembly times, reduced
manufacturing costs and the mounting of components on
both sides of the PCB, increasing design flexibility.
Hot riveting: Now largely consigned to history, hot
riveting was the primary method of joining iron and steel
structural plates used in the construction of buildings,
ships, bridges and pressure vessels for over a century,
before being superseded in the 1950s by welding and
structural bolts.
The rivet consisted of a pin (shank) with a head at one
end and was originally made from wrought iron and later
from low carbon steel. These materials are relatively soft
with good ductility and did not require tempering after
cooling.
While riveting was an efficient operation it was labour
intensive, typically requiring a skilled team of four
workers. Operations required the rivets to be heated in
a small furnace close to the installation site, usually with
the pin shank down, in order to keep the head cooler
and retain some strength during installation. When an
appropriate temperature was reached the rivet would be
removed from the heat with tongs by one member of the
team, and thrown a short distance to be caught by another
team member with a funnel, who would position the rivet
in the hole prepared in the plates to be joined, before
getting ready to catch the next rivet, A third team member
would force the rivet head against the plate surface while
a partner on the other side deformed the end with a
pneumatic hammer. As the rivet cooled and contracted a
strong, permanent, watertight joint was formed.
A wide range of different factory head styles was used depending
on the application. In the example provided, rivets with a
countersunk factory head were used, while a tool with a
concave end was used to produce a rounded shop head.
The slag stringers of the wrought iron allow the flow lines
to be seen in the rivets. Note the tight joint formed by the
rivets between the two wrought iron plates.
The riveted joint is a physical connection with no melting
or metallurgical bond being formed between any of the
components. While providing some strength in tension,
the riveted joint was strongest in shear, arising from both
the rivets themselves and the frictional contact that was
achieved between the plates joined. Rivets were typically
installed in groups with each rivet separated from its
neighbour by a distance of 3-5 shank diameters.

Metal Stitching: is not typically used as an initial
fabrication technique but was rather developed in the mid
1930s as a cold repair method to mend cracked or broken
cast iron, steel, bronze or other metal components without
welding. It involves inserting metal pins and locks into
carefully prepared holes along the fracture line, creating a
strong, pressure-tight repair, see Figure A4.1.h.
Metal stitching is particularly useful for parts where
welding is problematic or undesirable, such as cast
iron. Because it is a cold repair technique the original
metallurgy is maintained while distortion and stresses
associated with the heat of welding is avoided, while at the
same time offering restoration of the original components
strength.
Using a jig to guide placement, groups of holes are first
drilled across the fracture line up to 90% of a castings wall
thickness. These holes are then joined using sizing blades
to create a serrated aperture before a preformed nickel
alloy key is inserted using an interference fit. A second
group of holes is then drilled along the line of the fracture
and again joined and these holes filled with studs, each
stud biting into its predecessor, resulting in a pressure-
tight join and restoring rigidity to the casting. The studs
and keys are then ground down flush, completing the
repair.
Metal stitching has commonly found use in the
repair of engine blocks, bearing saddles, water jackets,
turbine casings and various other components in heavy
machinery.

TEMPORARY JOINING TECHNIQUES
Fastening or joining materials mechanically through the
use of screws, rivets, bolts, pins, clips, nails, press-studs
and snaps are considered temporary joining techniques.
Blind rivets: (also known as breakstem rivets and Pop
rivets) are typically used for the joining of sheet metal and
consist of a solid stem or mandrel terminating in a button
like head. A short tubular body or shaft with a flanged end
is positioned on the stem just below the head, as may be
seen in Figure A 4.1.9m.
This joining method is not intended for structural
applications,
Installation is achieved by inserting the shaft into a
prepared hole in the sheet metal being joined so that the
head of the mandrel protrudes from the other side. A
setting tool is then positioned over the mandrel stem such
that the flanged head of the shaft is held against the sheet
metal surface. With the aid of the setting tool, the mandrel
is pulled back through the tubular shaft. As this takes place,
the button head of the mandrel compresses and deforms
the end of the shaft between itself and the sheet metal
surface. Compression continues until the tensile strength
of the mandrel is exceeded and it fractures, leaving behind
a completed joint. Advantages:
Heat not involved
Single operator installation
« Can be installed from one side
Installation does not require high level of skill.
Disadvantages:
« Can only be used on relatively thin material
« Surfaces have to be perforated with a drill.
Bolts, Screws and nails: each of these rely on physical
interaction with the material being joined.
Nails remain in place by the pressure of the surrounding
fibres. Removal requires frictional contact forces to be
overcome.
Screws derive their fixation strength from a mechanical
bond in which pull-out requires the shearing of material
contained between the threads. As a screw is driven into
an assembly, the joint is placed in compression when the
head of the screw meets the surface.
Bolts act to hold two or more parts of an assembly together
by applying a clamping force between the bolt head and
nut. As the nut is tightened the bolt is placed in tension
and the joint assembly in compression. Bolts are available
in a variety of materials and strength levels and head
designs to match the situation.

Finishing techniques are often directed toward the
protection of surfaces by creating a barrier against
environmental factors that might degrade the visual
appearance and serviceability of a product. In addition,
finishing techniques can add to the visual aesthetic by
offering choice of texture and colour.
Anodising: anodising is an electrochemical process that
thickens the natural oxide layer on a metal surface, most
commonly aluminum, to produce a hard, durable and
corrosion-resistant finish. It is often used for decorative
purposes as the oxide layer is porous, which allows it to
absorb dyes and pigments, enabling a wide range of colors
and finishes. Unlike painting, anodising preserves the
metallic appearance of the metal.
Electro-plating: is a general description of a process
that involves depositing a metal coating onto a substrate
using electrolysis that can offer a range of finishes, from
shiny chrome to matte silver, and can even be textured or
polished to achieve specific visual effects.
Common metals used in electroplating include gold,
silver, copper, nickel, chromium, zinc, tin and platinum.
Recently, advancements have made it possible to
electroplate certain plastics after they are prepared with a
conductive coating.
Electroplating enhances a product’s aesthetics by providing
a decorative metallic finish that can increase visual appeal
and add a sense of luxury or quality. Decorative coatings
are typically only 5 - 15 microns (um) (0.005 - 0.015mm)
thick, while coatings applied for corrosion resistance may
be up to 0.5mm.
Galvanising: is a process of applying a protective zinc
coating to iron or steel to prevent rusting. This coating
acts as a barrier, preventing corrosive substances from
reaching the underlying metal.
Hot-dip galvanising is the most common and recognisable
galvanising process, and involves immersing steel in a
bath of molten zinc, maintained at a temperature of 445-
465°C. The process creates a durable coating that can be
matte grey in colour or exhibit a crystalline pattern, called
‘spangles. The spangle pattern is a natural outcome of the
zinc solidifying and forming distinct crystals as it cools
following removal from the bath, see Figure A 4.1.10a.
Spangle size and appearance are influenced by the cooling
rate, zinc bath composition and steel type. Coating
thicknesses are typically between 50 and 150 microns
(um), (0.05 to 0.15 mm).

CRAFT PRODUCTION
Before the Industrial Revolution, manufacturing, known
as craft production, was centred around local craftsmen
in medieval villages. Young apprentices, often serving
for as long as seven years, supported small, sometimes
home-based, workshops driven by skilled artisans. Most
manufacturing took place in cottages in rural villages.
Craft production refers to the process of creating goods by
hand or with minimal mechanisation, often emphasising
quality, uniqueness and traditional techniques. It involves
the creation of single, unique, and individual products,
generally requiring manual skills, where the skill of the
artisan determines the quality of one-off, unique pieces.
While slow, this method was often locally based and
allowed clients to converse directly with the manufacturer
to produce customised items. Because a craftsman’s
manufacturing involves a project from start to finish,
these skilled artisans had a variety of skills.
If machines were used, they were typically small and
generally operated by hand, animal power, wind or
running water. While windmills and water wheels had
been around for centuries, energy for craft production
was mostly provided by human muscle. In some instances,
earlier Roman-design water wheels were employed. Hand-
operated bellows forced air into the blacksmith’s forge (as
seen in Figure B 4.1.1a). Hand- or foot-operated spinning
wheels enabled the spinning of plant and animal fibres into
thread, while others wove the yarn into cloth. Timber was
fashioned with basic hand tools. Leather goods, clothing,
silverware and food products were all made similarly. Iron
and other metals were either hand forged or cast. Little
changed from one generation to the next as sons adopted
the role their fathers had previously occupied.
At this point in history, there was little need to generate
a surplus other than insurance against crop failure or
inclement weather. Neighbouring villages often produced
the same crops, so trade, while local, was still minimal.
The town’s craftsmen included bakers, blacksmiths
and carpenters. Villages, to a large degree, were self-
sufficient with products made from local materials,
manufactured on-site and repaired within the town. Much
of the machinery in use changed little until the Industrial
Revolution.
As transport routes were developed and improved, village
craftsmen such as weavers started to buy additional raw
materials to be spun into thread or woven into cloth and
sold to the emerging local merchants. Cottage labourers
also sold their work to merchants in an organised fashion
in a process that has been termed ‘proto-industrialisation’
Craft products soon became tradeable commodities, and
cottage workers who owned their equipment dealt directly
with the local market or used merchants to trade farther
afield. The opportunities of increased trade promised by
improved transport contributed to the need for changes
in the means of production. The main change involved
the mechanisation of processing and the centralisation of
production in large factories located in the cities. Many
smaller, more diversified operations (cottage industries)
were unable to compete and were closed. These changes
led to significant population shifts from rural areas to
cities.
Today, master craftsmen produce individually designed
pieces tailored to client requests. These pieces exhibit both
high quality and cost. Examples of this approach include:
haute couture (as seen in Figure B4.1.1b), jewellery and
customised furniture. One-off production may be used
for prototype development and testing before moving to
larger scales of production.
In developing economies, one-off production remains
viable due to the low cost of labour and the lack of
distribution infrastructure. In these situations, locally
based artisans meet the needs of local clients with
appropriate technologies and available materials,
Many countries, including Korea, Japan and China,
have recognised the importance of traditional crafts and
have designated highly skilled artisans as ‘living cultural
treasures’ to preserve these skills and technologies for
future generations. These artisans are often seen as living
embodiments of cultural heritage and play a crucial
role in passing down knowledge and skills through
apprenticeships.
These traditional skills are used for building maintenance,
repair and restoration work. An example of this can be
seen in the rebuilding of St George’s Hall roof at Windsor
Castle and, more recently, the reconstruction of Notre
Dame Cathedral after fire ravaged the structure.
raft production offers a range of distinctive advantages,
particularly in terms of product quality and individuality.
Each item is typically characterised by a high degree
of craftsmanship and uniqueness, often reflecting the
artisan’s personal skill and creative expression. This allows
for a high level of customisation, making it well-suited
to bespoke or specialised markets. Additionally, craft
production generally requires less energy than industrial
manufacturing processes and frequently incorporates
sustainable or locally sourced materials, aligning with
environmentally conscious practices.
Craft production is also associated with several inherent
limitations. The processesinvolvedare typicallylabour-and
time-intensive, which contribute to elevated production
costs and, consequently, higher market prices. The limited scale of output further constrains its competitiveness,
particularly when compared with the efficiency and cost-
effectiveness of mass production systems. As a result, craft
production tends to occupy a niche segment of the market,
appealing primarily to consumers who prioritise artisanal
value, exclusivity and sustainability over affordability and
scalability.
MECHANISED PRODUCTION
The centrepiece of the Industrial Revolution was the
availability of new sources of power and the accompanying
mechanisation. Before the Industrial Revolution, humans
or animals supplied power. James Watt's improved
Newcomen engine proved an effective and reliable power
source. Steam engines soon found applications in fields
other than mining, boosting productivity in the booming
textiles industry. While waterpower was still used as a
power source, steam engines provided more flexibility and
allowed industry to spread across the countryside, away
from sources of running water.
Mechanised production involves the introduction of
machinery into a process with the intent of removing
human and animal power sources. In its simplest form,
it replaces hand-crafting operations with machine-based
tooling. Machines replacing manual labour still require
human input to operate, control and often guide the
machines.
Mechanisation offers a range of significant advantages
within industrial and manufacturing contexts. It facilitates
the production of goods at reduced cost, with increased
speed, and with enhanced uniformity and quality. By
automating processes, mechanisation diminishes reliance
on manual labour, thereby enabling continuous operation
and significantly boosting productivity, particularly in
large-scale production environments. Moreover, the
mechanisation of hazardous tasks contributes to improved
occupational health and safety by mitigating workers’
exposure to dangerous conditions.
However, these benefits are accompanied by notable
drawbacks. The initial capital outlay required for
mechanised systems—encompassing plant infrastructure
and specialised equipment—is substantial. Additionally,
the transition to mechanised production often results
in workforce displacement, as traditional labour roles
are supplanted by a smaller cohort of technically skilled
personnel. Mechanisation may also constrain operational
flexibility, given that machines are typically designed for
specific, predefined tasks. Furthermore, the increased
energy demands associated with mechanised systems can
exacerbate environmental impacts, raising concerns about
sustainability and resource consumption.

Assembly line production
Assembly-line production is an industrial process used
in mass production. It involves the movement of the
product to be manufactured from station to station, often
using conveyor belts or other transportation systems.
The product moves past workers, tooling and machinery
stations until the product is complete. Often referred to as
‘reductionism, assembly line workers are typically involved
in work reduced to simple, repetitive tasks that had little
association with the production of a final product. People
worked to a ‘rhythm’ but were involved in tasks devoid of
any interest, thought or problem solving.
With production methods and operations standardised,
workers were often deskilled compared to the craftsmen
of previous times. As mechanisation increased, many of
the tasks simply became machine-tending.
While Henry Ford was not the first to use the assembly line,
he is credited with refining the controlled movement of
standardised parts and revolutionising mass production.
Labelled as ‘Fordism, this allowed the mass production of
vehicles of a consistent quality. Consumers at this point,
however, had their purchase options limited as defined by
Ford'’s legendary quote, “My customers can also get any
colour, as long as it’s black.” Figure B4.1.1d shows workers
on a Ford assembly line in 1930.
Assembly line production offers a range of strategic
advantages that have made it a cornerstone of modern
manufacturing. One of its most significant benefits is
the high level of efficiency and throughput it enables.
By breaking down the production process into smaller,
repetitive tasks, assembly lines allow for faster production
rates and shorter cycle times. This streamlined approach
ensures that each worker or machine focuses on a specific
function, thereby reducing delays and increasing overall
productivity. Another key advantage is the reduction in
unit costs. Assembly line systems are designed to support
mass production, which facilitates economies of scale. As
the volume of output increases, the cost per unit decreases,
making products more affordable for consumers and more
profitable for manufacturers. This cost-effectiveness is a
major driver behind the widespread adoption of assembly
line techniques in industries ranging from automotive to
electronics.
Consistency and standardisation are also key features of
assembly line production. Because each product follows
the same sequence of operations, manufacturers can
ensure uniform quality and reliability across large batches.
This level of consistency is important in sectors where
precision and dependability are critical. In addition,
assembly lines reduce the need for highly skilled labour.
Workers are trained to perform specific, narrowly defined
tasks. The training process is also simplified and less
time-consuming. This not only lowers labour costs but
also makes it easier to scale up operations quickly when
demand increases.
Finally, the linear and organised structure of assembly
lines contributes to improved workflow and operational
oversight. The clear sequence of tasks facilitates better
coordination, easier supervision and more efficient use
of space and resources. This structured environment
supports continuous improvement and helps identify
bottlenecks or inefficiencies in the production process.

HYBRID PRODUCTION
A hybrid production system represents a strategic
integration of craft, mechanised and automated production
methods, designed to leverage the unique strengths of
each while mitigating their respective limitations. This
approach allows manufacturers to specify a process that
balances sometimes conflicting needs. Considerations
such as flexibility, efficiency, and quality make this
approach particularly well-suited to the demands of
modern, rapidly changing markets.
A key feature of hybrid production is its ability to
combine the customisation and adaptability of craft
production with the speed, precision and consistency
offered by mechanised and automated systems. This
enables manufacturers to produce both standardised
and bespoke products efficiently, catering to a broader
range of consumer needs without sacrificing quality or
responsiveness.
Human-machine collaboration is another feature of hybrid
systems. Here, workers are typically assigned tasks that
require creativity, fine motor skills, or complex decision-
making, while machines handle repetitive, high-volume,
or precision-driven dirty or dangerous operations. This
division of labour enhances productivity, preserves the
value of human craftsmanship and assures the appropriate
health and safety in areas where OH&S' issues may arise.
Hybrid systems are also inherently scalable and adaptable.
They can be configured to support large-scale mass
production or adjusted for small-batch and custom
manufacturing, responding to market demand.
Hybrid production systems often incorporate advanced
technologies and robotics. Enabling real-time data
collection, predictive analytics and intelligent automation,
creating a data-driven production environment. Hybrid
systems, therefore, may improve efficiency and enhance
strategic planning across the production lifecycle.
Hybrid manufacturing systems offer a blend of benefits,
including the ability to balance cost and quality. The
strategic application of automation, where it is most
efficient while retaining human input where precision or
creativity is required, can deliver high-quality products
without incurring the full costs associated with either
extreme.
A key benefit is the system’s ability to respond to market
changes. Hybrid systems are more easily reconfigured
than fully automated lines, allowing manufacturers
to respond to shifts in consumer demand or product
specifications. The system’s capacity to support a wide
variety of products makes it ideal for industries requiring
frequent customisation or product variation.
Resource efficiency is also another feature of hybrid
manufacturing. Through the considered allocation of tasks
between humans and machines, reduced waste, improved
energy use, and streamlined production processes are
achieved. These benefits support sustainability goals and
contribute to long-term cost savings.
Hybrid systems are not without their challenges.
Designing and managing such systems is inherently
complex, requiring careful coordination between diverse
technologies and workflows. The initial setup costs can
be substantial, particularly when integrating advanced
technologies like robotics, AI*, or IoT” infrastructure.
Hybrid systems also demand a highly skilled workforce
capable of operating both manual and automated
components. This requires a technically proficient
workforce with the ability to maintain and adapt to
evolving technologies. Integration of multiple manual
and automated processes can generate issues of efficiency
and compatibility if not carefully selected and tuned.
The benefits attributed to this approach can enly be
achieved through seamless communication and workflow
continuity across the range of system components
employed.

The design for manufacturing process canbe deconstructed
into three complementary components: design for process
(materials and methods), design for assembly, and design
for disassembly.
Design for process involves reductions in: the amount
of energy consumed, the number of production
processes employed, waste generated and emissions
produced. Designers may simplify the design to make the
manufacturing process easier and more efficient, It may
also involve reducing the number of parts that require
additional operations, including: plating, painting,
printing, labelling, insertion of screws, caulking, riveting
and welding.
Specifying standardised components reduces costs,
improves availability and makes repairs and maintenance
easier, Adopting the principles of design for process at the
design stage provides a framework for selecting materials
and fastening techniques, as well as designing modular
assemblies, The selection of appropriate materials reduces
toxic substances, hazardous waste and polluting emissions,
as well as the quantity of materials required. Wherever
possible, single-component materials may be specified
for moulding and recyclable materials identified and
marked for sorting and recycling. These measures assist
in the later disassembly and recycling processes. Similar
specifications are also applied to packaging.

of design for process is the Apple Unibody MacBook.
The MacBook body was carefully designed to allow a

did not require the body to join or weld parts together.
This single structure is stronger and more rigid than

Design for assembly is an approach used by designers to
analyse components and sub-assemblies, reducing costs
by minimising the number of parts and maximising the
efficiency of assembly processes. Fewer parts most often
translates into faster assembly times. A by-product of part
reduction is also smaller inventories and lower storage
costs for manufacturers.
Consideration of tools to be used during assembly
may also be important. A reduction in the number and
variety of fasteners and tool types used during assembly
is preferable. Better product value and longevity can be
embedded at the design stage using modular design
principles. Modular design involves breaking down
complex systems into sets of distinct, independent
components or sub-assemblies that can then be assembled
in various ways. Replaceable modules enable assemblies to
be removed for part replacement, due to failure, or when
modification by updating is required. Waste can also be
reduced if a large product does not have to be scrapped
because a small part fails or becomes obsolete.
An example of a product redesigned using design for
assembly principles is the Bosch circular saw. The original
Bosch circular consisted of more than 100 parts. Assembly
was time-consuming and often error-prone. The assembly
was complicated by the fact that many parts required
manual alignment and fastening.
Using design for assembly principles, several key changes
were made. The part count was reduced by combining
multiple components into a single moulded part. This
also reduced the number of fasteners required, and snap-
fit connections were integrated. The assembly process
was further simplified by the inclusion of self-locating,
symmetrical parts to prevent incorrect assembly. A
vertical axis of assembly was employed, enabling parts
to be dropped into place. Custom fasteners were also
replaced with standard screws and connectors.

Design for manufacturing conflicts
On occasions, design for manufacturing approaches may
be in conflict. In every case, a holistic assessment may be
required to determine the most advantageous result.
Materials selection: The materials chosen for
manufacturing operations can present challenges for
disassembly and recycling. In the automotive industry,
aluminium is often specified for body panels due to
its lightweight nature, corrosion resistance and ease of
formability. During the product use phase, lighter vehicles
also benefit from improved fuel economy, reduced
emissions and decreased brake wear. However, despite
aluminium’s 100% recyclability, its disassembly can be
complex, and the environmental issues associated with the
aluminium smelting process pose a significant conflict.
Reducing complexity: In pursuit of design simplification,
manufacturers frequently consolidate components into
integrated assemblies. While this aggregation streamlines
production, it often introduces greater complexity and
reduces accessibility when attempting to disassemble the
component into its constituent parts.
Joining methods: Permanent joining techniques are widely
recognised for their speed, effectiveness and efficiency in
manufacturing processes. However, these methods often
pose significant challenges to disassembly, sometimes
rendering the separation of components impractical
or unfeasible. This issue is particularly prevalent in the
furniture industry, where the extensive use of adhesives
and glues impedes efforts to disassemble products for
recycling, maintenance or repair.
Cost: Designing cost-effective solutions that align with
design for manufacturing principles presents a complex
challenge. In the construction sector, modular building
systems offer notable advantages, including streamlined
assembly processes, mitigation of weather-related delays
and reduced overall construction timelines. However,
integration of features that enable disassembly—essential
for adaptability, maintenance or end-of-life recycling—
can introduce additional design complexity and elevate
production costs, potentially offsetting the economic
benefits of modularity.

Designers may employ various strategies when using a
design for assembly approach.
Modular sub-assemblies reduce the number of parts by
combining the function of multiple pieces into a single
standardised component or module. This may result in
assembly efficiencies and lower inventories. Minimising
part numbers frequently reduces overall product weight.
Modern construction methods producing modular
designs include everything from ships (utilising block
construction and prefabrication to divide ships into
functional interchangeable modules that are assembled
on land and then integrated for construction and future
upgrades) to computer modules and home construction.
Effective and efficient product assembly minimises the
number of axes of assembly. Wherever possible, assembly
operations should be in one direction, reducing handling,
time-consuming movement and re-orientation of the
product. The item should be easy to assemble manually,
and where the scale of production warrants it, with
automation. Automated assembly processes are more
consistent, produce a more reliable product and generate
a consistently higher build quality. To assist with assembly,
locating pins should be chamfered or tapered.
Standardising parts means readily available off-the-shelf
components are specified, which makes procurement
easier and may reduce the need for large inventories.
Building bricks, nuts and bolts, electronic parts such as
resistors, etc, are all examples of considerations in this
context.
Design for accessibility ensures that all parts are easily
accessible during assembly, reducing the need for complex
tools or maneuvers.
Minimising and simplifying fastening methods through
the use of snap-fittings, clips, etc, enables assembly and
disassembly without the need for special tools. A reduction
in fasteners not only reduces costs but also assembly and
repair times. If fasteners are specified, standard types and
sizes should be used.

alternatives that do not compromise product
functionality. If removal of hazardous materials is not
possible, then measures must be put in place for end-
of-life recovery and/or recycling.
