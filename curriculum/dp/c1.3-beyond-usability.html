A product can be perfectly usable and still be one you would never choose. Usability is a floor rather than a goal, and once a market is full of things that all work, the question stops being whether a product functions and becomes why anyone would want this one. C1.3 takes that question seriously instead of dismissing it as marketing.

The two frameworks here, the four pleasures and the ACT model, are useful for a reason students sometimes miss. They give you language for reactions that would otherwise sound like personal taste. Being able to say that an object delivers socio-pleasure, rather than just saying it feels nice, is what turns a preference into an argument, and arguments are what get marked. This topic also rewards being tested against things you already own. Work out why you are attached to a particular object when a cheaper one would do the same job, and you will understand this material better than any set of notes can make you.

Students must be able toOutline how the four-pleasure framework can be used to create designs that intentionally trigger socio-, physio-, psycho- and ideo-pleasure responses.

A product can be usable and still be unappealing. Going beyond usability means designing products that are not just functional but genuinely pleasurable to use. The Four Pleasures Framework, developed by Canadian anthropologist Lionel Tiger (1992), categorises the pleasure derived from products into four distinct types:

  • Physio-pleasure: pleasure from physical sensations: how a product feels, smells, tastes, or sounds. Example: the smooth, ribbed grip of a cold Coca-Cola bottle feels secure and satisfying in the hand. Its organic shape and nostalgic design reinforce this sensory appeal.
  • Socio-pleasure: pleasure from social connections, status, belonging, and communication. Example: Philippe Starck's Juicy Salif lemon squeezer is famously difficult to use successfully, yet it became a design classic because it sparks conversation and signals sophistication to others.
  • Psycho-pleasure: pleasure from cognitive and emotional experiences: understanding and mastering a product. Example: a well-designed interface that responds predictably to inputs gives the user a sense of control and competence.
  • Ideo-pleasure: pleasure from values, beliefs, and meaning. Example: choosing a product made from recycled ocean plastics because it aligns with the user's environmental values.

These four pleasures help designers consider the full emotional experience of using a product, not just whether it works. The framework aligns directly with Donald Norman's three levels of design (2003):

  • Visceral level: immediate sensory reaction (appearance, feel, sound). Corresponds to physio-pleasure.
  • Behavioural level: functional usability and ease of use. Corresponds to psycho-pleasure.
  • Reflective level: meaning, personal value, and social identity. Corresponds to socio-pleasure and ideo-pleasure.

The Juicy Salif demonstrates why the framework matters: it fails at the behavioural/psycho level (poor usability) yet succeeds dramatically at the reflective level (socio- and ideo-pleasure). As Starck allegedly said: "My juicer is not meant to squeeze lemons; it is meant to start conversations." Without the Four Pleasures Framework, a designer might dismiss this product as a failure when it is actually satisfying a different (but equally real) kind of need.

Physio-pleasure draws directly on the sensory and anthropometric factors (touch, sound, fit) introduced in A1.1 Ergonomics.

The ACT model (Attract, Converse, Transact) was developed by Trevor Van Gorp and Edie Adams (2012). It describes a sequential process through which designers can intentionally trigger emotional responses in users, moving them from initial awareness through engagement to lasting loyalty.

The three stages and their alignment with Norman's levels:

  • Attract: uses aesthetics (colour, texture, sound, smell) to grab the user's attention and create desire. Corresponds to Norman's Visceral level. A product that looks cheap, feels flimsy, or sounds hollow will fail here before the user has any interaction with it. Design strategies: high-contrast colours, premium materials, satisfying initial sounds.
  • Converse: ensures the product is usable, reliable, and gives the user a sense of control. Corresponds to Norman's Behavioural level. The product must "converse" predictably with the user: responding to inputs as expected, providing clear feedback, and completing tasks efficiently without frustration. Design strategies: clear affordances, consistent feedback, familiar population stereotypes (e.g., turning a knob clockwise for "on").
  • Transact: builds deeper meaning and emotional connection, leading to continued use, brand loyalty, and recommendation to others. Corresponds to Norman's Reflective level. This stage addresses socio-pleasure (community, status) and ideo-pleasure (values alignment). Design strategies: personalisation options (changeable watch faces), community-building features (user forums, fitness leaderboards), and values-aligned features (a carbon savings dashboard on an electric vehicle).

Smartwatch example applying both frameworks:

  • Attract (Visceral / Physio-pleasure): Sleek design, smooth curves, vibrant edge-to-edge display, premium aluminium or titanium body.
  • Converse (Behavioural / Psycho-pleasure): Intuitive touch interface, reliable health tracking, fast and predictable navigation through menus.
  • Transact (Reflective / Socio- and Ideo-pleasure): Changeable watch faces for self-expression; fitness data connects the user to a community of like-minded people; the watch becomes part of the user's identity and daily routine, generating loyalty and upgrade behaviour.

The frameworks working together: The Four Pleasures Framework tells designers what kinds of pleasure to aim for. The ACT model tells designers in what order to deliver them. Usability (the Behavioural/Psycho layer) is necessary but not sufficient. Products that users truly love must attract attention, sustain meaningful interaction, and build lasting emotional connection.

Material choice is one of the most direct ways to trigger physio- or ideo-pleasure at the Attract stage; see B3.1 Material Selection for how aesthetic and sustainable material choices are justified.

Products we love vs. products that just work

Think of one product you use constantly but feel nothing about: it works, you'd notice if it broke, and that's the whole relationship. Now think of one product you'd defend in an argument, recommend unprompted, or feel a flicker of pride owning.

Map both against the Four Pleasures Framework and the ACT model. Where exactly does the second product pick up something the first one never even attempts? Q1 · 1.3.1 Four Pleasures Framework
What does the four-pleasure framework give a designer that a usability checklist does not?
Usability establishes whether a product can be operated. The four pleasures, proposed by the anthropologist Lionel Tiger and later applied to products, cover why a user wants to keep using it: physio-pleasure from the senses and the body, socio-pleasure from what it signals to others, psycho-pleasure from feeling competent, and ideo-pleasure from matching a user's values. Naming them turns a reaction that would be dismissed as personal taste into a design decision that can be defended.
Q2 · 1.3.1 Four Pleasures Framework
The ribbed grip of a cold glass bottle, secure and satisfying in the hand, delivers:
Physio-pleasure arrives through the body and the senses: touch, grip, temperature, weight, sound and smell. Nothing social or intellectual is involved in the feel of the bottle itself, which is why this maps onto Norman's visceral level.
Q3 · 1.3.1 Four Pleasures Framework
A buyer chooses a particular watch because of what owning it signals to colleagues. This is:
Socio-pleasure comes from relationships, belonging and the standing a product confers. The purchase is being made for what the object says to other people rather than for how it feels in the hand or how well it keeps time.
Q4 · 1.3.1 Four Pleasures Framework
An interface that responds predictably to every input, so a new user feels in control and competent within minutes, is delivering:
Psycho-pleasure is cognitive and emotional: the satisfaction of understanding a product and mastering it. It corresponds to Norman's behavioural level, and it is the layer most closely tied to conventional usability, which is exactly why usability alone is a floor rather than a goal.
Q5 · 1.3.1 Four Pleasures Framework
Choosing a product made from recycled ocean plastic because it matches the buyer's environmental beliefs is an example of:
Ideo-pleasure comes from a product agreeing with the user's values and beliefs, independent of how it looks or performs. Material choice is one of the most direct ways to trigger it, which is why sustainability claims sit alongside aesthetics in a material justification.
Q6 · 1.3.2 The ACT Model
Which of Donald Norman's three levels covers the immediate sensory reaction to how something looks, feels and sounds?
The visceral level is the pre-conscious first reaction, and it aligns with physio-pleasure and with the Attract stage of the ACT model. Behavioural covers use and performance, and reflective covers meaning, values and identity.
Q7 · 1.3.2 The ACT Model
The Converse stage of the ACT model aligns with which level and pleasure?
Converse is the stage of actually using the product, so it depends on clear affordances, consistent feedback and familiar population stereotypes such as turning a knob clockwise for on. If the product cannot hold a predictable conversation with the user, no amount of attraction will keep them.
Q8 · 1.3.2 The ACT Model
The Transact stage of the ACT model refers to:
Transact is the long-term relationship, built through personalisation, community features and values-aligned details such as a carbon savings display. Despite its name it is not the sale: it is the reflective level, where socio-pleasure and ideo-pleasure turn a customer into an advocate.
Q9 · 1.3.2 The ACT Model
The Juicy Salif lemon squeezer is difficult to use yet remains a design classic. In framework terms it:
As a juicer it performs poorly, so psycho-pleasure is weak. What it delivers is socio-pleasure, in the conversations and sophistication it signals, and ideo-pleasure for buyers who share its design philosophy. Without the framework a designer would score it a failure, when it is actually meeting a different and equally real need. It is an exception rather than a model for a phone or a medical device.
Q10 · 1.3.2 The ACT Model
A retailer offers free one-to-one technical help that has nothing to do with that day's purchase. Which ACT stage does this serve?
Support given independently of a sale builds the ongoing relationship that turns a customer into an advocate, which is the reflective payoff of the Transact stage. In the same space, an uncluttered glass storefront does the attracting, and staff carrying the point-of-sale system so that no queue ever forms keeps the conversation moving.

Question 1 · C1.3 · HL only6 marks
Case study

Vinyl records outsold CDs again in 2022, forty years after the format was declared obsolete. A record must be removed from its sleeve, placed on a turntable, and the stylus lowered onto it. It plays for about twenty minutes before it must be turned over. It is easily scratched and cannot be carried.

The same music is available instantly on a streaming service for a monthly fee.

Table 1: Vinyl compared with streaming

Vinyl LPStreaming
Cost per album£28Included in £11 per month
Time to start playing~60 s~2 s
Uninterrupted playing time~20 min per sideUnlimited
PortabilityNoneComplete
Sleeve artwork310 mm squareThumbnail
OwnershipPhysical objectLicence, revocable

(a) State which of the four pleasures is served by the 310 mm sleeve artwork, see Table 1. [1]

(b) Outline how the physical handling of a record serves the physio-pleasure, see Table 1. [2]

(c) Explain why vinyl's usability failures in Table 1 have not prevented its return. [3]

Example answer

(a) Psycho-pleasure, from the visual and emotional response to the artwork at that size.

(b) Physio-pleasure comes from the senses and the body, and playing a record is a sequence of deliberate physical actions: sliding the disc from its sleeve, feeling its weight and the texture of the groove, lowering the stylus. The sound itself has a physical component too, since a large speaker moving air produces a bass the listener feels as well as hears.

(c) Usability measures how efficiently a product lets a user reach a goal, and it assumes the goal is to get to the music. For a vinyl buyer the handling is not an obstacle before the experience, it is part of the experience, so the things that count as failures on a usability measure are the source of the value. The twenty-minute side is the clearest case: it looks like a defect because it interrupts listening, and in practice it enforces attention, because the listener must return to the turntable and therefore treats the album as a thing to sit with rather than as background. The 310 mm sleeve and the physical object also do something streaming cannot, since owning a record means possessing an object that can be shelved, lent and displayed, which is a socio-pleasure a revocable licence cannot deliver. What the case really shows is that usability is necessary for a tool and insufficient for a product bought for the experience of using it, which is exactly the gap the four-pleasure framework was developed to describe.

Question 2 · C1.3 · HL only6 marks
Case study

A city museum issues a handheld audio guide. A visitor takes it at the entrance, keys in the number displayed beside an object, and hears a two-minute commentary. Numbers appear on about 90 of the museum's 4000 objects.

An audit found that most visitors used the guide for the first four objects and then stopped, and that a third returned it before reaching the second floor.

Table 2: The guide mapped against the ACT model

StageWhat the guide doesObserved outcome
AttractOffered at the desk with the ticket62 % take one
ConverseVisitor keys in a number, listensMedian 4 objects used
TransactVisitor completes a route through the museum33 % return it early

(a) State the stage of the ACT model at which the guide is failing most, see Table 2. [1]

(b) Describe why keying in a number weakens the conversation between visitor and guide, see Table 2. [2]

(c) Evaluate the audio guide against the ACT model, see Table 2. [3]

Example answer

(a) Converse.

(b) A conversation implies the product responds to the person, and keying in a number is the visitor doing all the work while the guide waits: it knows nothing about where the visitor is, what they have already heard or what interests them. The interaction also interrupts looking, because the visitor must break away from the object to find a number and press keys, so the guide competes with the exhibit instead of supporting it.

(c) Attract works. Offering the guide with the ticket at the point of entry reaches 62 % of visitors, and a physical object handed over is hard to decline, so the guide gets into more hands than a leaflet or an app would. Converse is where it fails, and the median of four objects shows the failure is immediate rather than gradual: visitors are not tiring of it, they are abandoning it almost at once, because the effort of keying in numbers is repaid with a fixed two-minute commentary that does not adapt. Transact fails as a consequence, since a third return the guide before the second floor, so the guide is not carrying visitors through the museum, which is the outcome the museum wanted from it. Numbers on 90 of 4000 objects also mean the guide is silent for almost everything a visitor looks at, so the conversation stops even when the visitor is willing. The judgment is that the guide is well distributed and poorly designed, and improving Attract further would only put more of them into the hands of people who abandon them.

Question 3 · C1.3 · HL only10 marks
Case study · part 1

A publisher produces a cooperative board game. Two to four players work together against the game itself, so everyone wins or everyone loses. A session lasts about an hour.

The box contains a folding board, wooden pieces, cards and a rulebook. The rules take about fifteen minutes to teach.

(a) Identify two aspects of the game that serve the socio-pleasure. [2]

Case study · part 2

The publisher tested two versions of the pieces.

Version AVersion B
PiecesPrinted card discsTurned beech, painted
Cost per copy£3.10£7.40
Retail price supported£24£42
Playtester rating, "want to own"5.8 / 108.9 / 10
Playtester rating, "easy to play"7.9 / 108.0 / 10

(b) Outline what the gap between the two ratings in Table 3 shows about usability and desire. [2]

Case study · part 3

The box lid carries a large illustration and is displayed face out on a shop shelf beside forty other games.

(c) Describe how the box lid serves the attract stage of the ACT model, see Table 3. [2]

Case study · part 4

The publisher must decide between version A and version B before printing.

(d) Explain how the four-pleasure framework and the ACT model together support a decision between the two versions, see Table 3. [4]

Example answer

(a) The cooperative structure, which makes players discuss and plan together rather than compete; and the fifteen-minute teaching time, which lets an experienced player bring newcomers into a group.

(b) The two versions are almost identical on ease of play, 7.9 against 8.0, so the wooden pieces have not made the game work better in any functional sense. On wanting to own it the gap is three points, which shows that what the pieces changed was desire rather than performance, and that the two are independent: a product can be entirely usable and still be something nobody particularly wants.

(c) The lid is the only part of the product a shopper can see, since the game itself is sealed inside, so it has to do the whole of the attract stage on its own. Standing among forty competitors it must first be noticed at a distance, which is what the large illustration is for, and then say quickly what kind of experience is inside, so that the right buyer picks it up and the conversation with the product can begin.

(d) The two frameworks answer different halves of the decision, and together they point to version B.

The ACT model deals with the shelf. A game sealed in a box is bought on the lid alone, so attract is decided before anyone plays, and version B's price of £42 places it among games buyers expect to be substantial. A £24 game that looks premium is a mismatch, and the buyer who wants a serious cooperative game may pass over a cheap-looking box entirely. Converse and transact are unaffected by the choice, since the ratings show both versions play equally well, so ACT gives a reason to prefer B and no reason to prefer A.

The four pleasures explain why the wooden pieces produce the three-point gap. Physio-pleasure is the direct one: turned beech has weight, texture and a sound when set down that printed card does not, and a board game is handled constantly for an hour, so the pieces are touched more than anything else in the box. Ideo-pleasure follows, because wood carries associations of craft and permanence, and a buyer choosing a cooperative game is already choosing against disposability. Socio-pleasure matters most for this particular game: the pieces sit on a table between people for an hour and are what everyone looks at while talking, so a component that feels good to handle raises the quality of a shared occasion rather than an individual experience.

Set against the usability data, the case is clear. The 0.1 difference in ease of play means the extra £4.30 buys nothing functional, so a purely usability-driven decision would choose A. That is exactly the judgment these frameworks exist to correct, because they show the £4.30 is buying pleasure and desire, and the market rewards it with £18 more retail price.

Version B should be chosen. The one real risk is that it moves the game into a higher price bracket where it competes with more established titles, so the higher cost has to be recovered through a market that will pay £42, which the playtester rating of 8.9 supports but does not prove.

Case Study: ACT in a Retail Space

The smartwatch example above applies the ACT model to a single product. Apple applies the exact same three stages to an entire room. The all-glass storefront and minimalist facade are pure Attract: visible from the streetvisible from the street, deliberately uncluttered compared to a typical mall retailer, signalling premium simplicity before a customer has touched anything.

Converse happens the moment a customer steps inside: staff carry the point-of-sale system on a handheld device, so there's no queue, no counter, and no moment where the interaction stalls waiting for a till. Every product on the tables is powered on and usable rather than boxed behind glass, so a visitor can test it immediately and get a predictable, immediate response, exactly what the Behavioural level demands. Transact is the Genius Bar itself: free one-on-one technical help that has nothing to do with that day's purchase decision builds the kind of ongoing relationship that turns a customer into an advocate, the Reflective-level payoff the smartwatch achieves through watch faces and fitness communities.
