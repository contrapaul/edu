# Paper 2 Style Guide and Question-Writing Instructions

**DP Design Technology, first examinations 2027**

This document tells a teacher or an AI how to write a mock Paper 2, including the markscheme, so that it matches the only official material that exists for the new course. Follow it literally.

Read **[paper1.md](paper1.md) §4** first. The case study guidance there applies to Paper 2 as well and is only extended, not repeated, here.

## 3. Structure

### 3.1 The shape of the paper

**SL Paper 2 is seven case studies of 6, 6, 6, 6, 6, 10 and 10 marks.**

**HL Paper 2 is the same body of work at 50 marks, plus a 30-mark Design Thinking Challenge.**

This is the single most useful structural fact about the new Paper 2, and it comes straight from comparing the two specimens. The HL paper is not a longer SL paper with harder questions bolted on throughout. It is an SL-shaped 50-mark section, with two of the mid-paper case studies swapped for HL-content ones, followed by a three-question design challenge worth 30 marks.

### 3.4 The mark-tariff ladder

Both papers build each 6-mark case study on the same ladder, and it is remarkably consistent:

```
[1] one recall or identification point, in context
[2] a short account, two creditable points
[3] a reasoned or judged response, three creditable points
```

The two 10-mark case studies use:

```
[2] [2] [2] [4]
```

where the closing [4] is always the AO3 item: `Explain`, `Evaluate`.

There is no 5-mark or 7-mark short-answer item anywhere in the specimen. Tariffs are 1, 2, 3, 4, and then in the HL challenge only, 5, 6 and 8. **Do not invent tariffs outside this set.**

### 3.5 Answer boxes

The specimen gives ruled dotted lines inside a box, at **two lines per mark**:

| Tariff | Ruled lines |
| --- | --- |
| [1] | 2 |
| [2] | 4 |
| [3] | 6 |
| [4] | 8 |

Extended-response and drawing items get a full or half page. Blank pages carry:

> Please do not write on this page.
> Answers written on this page will not be marked.

## 4. Command terms

The 2027 guide restricts examinations to fifteen command terms, grouped by assessment objective. Reproduce these definitions exactly if you are writing a student-facing revision sheet.

### AO1 — Demonstrate knowledge

| Term | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| **Label** | Add a title, labels or brief explanation(s) to a diagram or graph. |
| **List** | Give a sequence of brief answers with no explanation. |
| **State** | Give a specific name, value or other brief answer without explanation or calculation. |

### AO2 — Understand and apply knowledge

| Term | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| **Apply** | Use an idea, equation, principle, theory or law in relation to a given problem or issue. |
| **Demonstrate** | Make clear by reasoning or evidence, illustrating with examples or practical application. |
| **Describe** | Give a detailed account. |
| **Identify** | Provide an answer from a number of possibilities. |
| **Outline** | Give a brief account or summary. |
| **Present** | Offer for display, observation, examination or consideration. |

### AO3 — Construct, analyse and evaluate

| Term | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| **Analyse** | Break down in order to bring out the guiding elements or structure. |
| **Construct** | Display information in a diagrammatic or logical form. |
| **Design** | Produce a plan, simulation or model. |
| **Evaluate** | Make an appraisal by weighing up the strengths and limitations. |
| **Explain** | Give a detailed account including reasons or causes. |
| **Justify** | Give valid reasons or evidence to support an answer or conclusion. |

### 4.1 Observed tariffs

| Command term | Tariffs used in the specimen | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| State | [1] | Always 1. Never more. |
| Identify | [1], [2] | [2] when the stem names two criteria, e.g. "based on cost and sustainability" |
| List | [2] | Always paired with "List **two** ..." |
| Label | [2] | Attached to a figure |
| Outline | [2] | Always 2 |
| Describe | [2] | Always 2 |
| Present | [2] | Rare. Used once, for anthropometric data |
| Demonstrate | [2] | Rare. Used once, for the ACT model |
| Apply | [2] | Used in the compound form `Apply the theory of X to describe why ...` |
| Analyse | [3] | Also drives the HL challenge product analysis at [3]+[3] |
| Justify | [3] | Stem names the factors to be justified, one mark each |
| Evaluate | [3], [4], [8] | [3] at SL, [4] for prototype comparison, [8] for the HL challenge evaluation |
| Explain | [3], [4] | [3] in a 6-mark case, [4] as the closing item of a 10-mark case |
| Construct | [6] | HL challenge only, problem statement |
| Design | [5] | HL challenge only, annotated diagram |

### 4.2 Rules for using command terms

1. **One command term per part.** The exception is the compound `Apply ... to describe ...` in HL 4(c), where `Apply` sets the theory and `describe` sets the output.
2. **The command term is the first word of the question**, after any scene-setting sentence. The specimen sometimes puts a sentence of context before it:
   > **(b)** Anthropometric data is used to determine the appropriate dimensions for user centered design (UCD).
   > **Outline** the percentile range for infants when deciding the length of the MOM incubator, see Figure 16 and Figure 17. **[2]**
3. **Quantify where the mark tariff implies a count.** `List two formal drawing techniques` [2]. `Identify two user-centred design (UCD) usability objectives` [2]. `Describe one way the designer of the MOM considered infant safety` [2].
4. **Never use a term outside the list.** `Define`, `Discuss`, `Suggest`, `Comment`, `Compare`, `Distinguish`, `Deduce` all appear in legacy papers and are **not** in the 2027 glossary.
5. **Match the term to the AO you are targeting.** The [3], [4], [5], [6] and [8] items carry `Analyse`, `Evaluate`, `Explain`, `Justify`, `Construct` and `Design`. Do not, however, distort the tariff ladder trying to hit the guide's stated 50/50 AO split on a command-term count. See §4.4.

### 4.4 A note on the AO weighting

The guide states an approximate 50/50 split between AO1+AO2 and AO3. Map the specimen's command terms onto the guide's own AO groupings and you get **60/40, not 50/50**, at both levels. The arithmetic is forced by the tariff ladder: a [1][2][3] case is 3 marks AO1/AO2 and 3 AO3, a [1][1][2][2] case is 6 and 0, and a [2][2][2][4] case is 6 and 4. The SL specimen's seven questions therefore give 30 marks of AO1/AO2 against 20 of AO3.

This is a classification question, not a structural one. The IB evidently counts some [2] items toward AO3, which is defensible for items requiring inference from supplied data rather than retrieval: reading a survey chart, matching design changes to user capabilities, judging which usability objective applies. Reclassifying the clearest of those brings the specimen close to 50/50.

**The instruction that follows from this: match the specimen's tariff structure and the AO weighting looks after itself.** Do not add a fifth part to a 6-mark case, or invent a [5] short-answer item, in pursuit of a 50/50 command-term count. Record both figures in your teacher notes and say which mapping you used.

### 4.3 Mark placement

Marks go in square brackets, right-aligned at the end of the question text: `... see Figure 3.[1]`. In the specimen there is often no space before the bracket when the question runs to the end of the line, and the bracket sits on its own at the far right when it does not. Use `[n]` right-aligned.

## 5. Case studies in Paper 2

Everything in [paper1.md §4](paper1.md) applies. Paper 2 adds four things.

### 5.1 Every question is a product analysis

The guide says Paper 2 is "based on the analysis of a product". In the specimen there is **no question that is not attached to a specific product, project or system**. Question 3 comes closest to abstraction, opening with a general statement about multidisciplinary design teams and the design thinking process, and even it lands immediately on a specific jar opener with a specific gear system, and every one of its parts is about that jar opener.

The rule for a mock is the same as for Paper 1 but stricter: **if a candidate could answer your question having never read the stimulus, rewrite it.**

Test each part by deletion. Take specimen HL 3(b):

> Outline why the multidisciplinary team designed the jar opener around the constraint of this gear system. [2]

Delete the case and it becomes meaningless. That is the target.

Compare a badly written version: "Outline two advantages of rack and pinion gears. [2]" Same content, same syllabus point, zero anchoring. This is the most common failure in teacher-written mocks.

### 5.2 Stimulus types the specimen uses

Paper 2 stimulus is richer than Paper 1's. Observed types:

| Type | Specimen example |
| --- | --- |
| Photograph of a real product, several views | MOM incubator, Figures 16–18 |
| Photograph pairs showing **design iterations** | MOM early prototype (Figures 19–21) vs later prototype (Figures 22–24) |
| Annotated student CAD from a real IA | Yoga organizer, Figure 2, with the student's own callouts ("Cut out handle reduces the number of parts and cost", "Mats stored horizontally are now easier to remove and return") |
| A student's specification table | Table 1, Essential / Desirable columns |
| Cutaway or component diagram | Jar opener gear system (Figure 5), carbon fibre tube structure (Figure 10) |
| Screenshot of a real interface | Airbnb listing (Figure 6), Giggle Greetings website (Figure 13) |
| Process infographic | Design thinking process (Figure 4), rainwater harvesting system (Figures 28–30) |
| Persona table | Loma village personas, Figure 27 |
| Constraints and resources tables | Figures 31–32 |
| Analytical framework as a figure | ACCESS FM, Figure 33 |
| Simulated user feedback table | Figure 34 |
| A photograph annotated with **mechanical or structural explanation** | Boeing wing over the hotel pool, Figures 7–8 |

The IA-derived stimulus deserves emphasis. Two of the seven specimen case studies at both levels draw on real DP student project work, anonymized. This is a deliberate signal about the relationship between IA and the exam. **Mock papers should use your own students' anonymized past project work as stimulus.** It is authentic, it is free, and it is unusually motivating.

### 5.3 Reusing a case study across parts

The specimen's 10-mark case studies build in stages, adding figures and text between parts rather than front-loading everything. Question 6 (Giggle Greetings) does this well:

1. Company framing + logo + website screenshot → (a) drawing techniques
2. New paragraph on sustainability and the AxiDraw purchase + Figure 14 → (b) motion types
3. New figure showing the plotter mechanism, Figure 15 → (c) accuracy
4. New paragraph on growth, seasonality and personalization → (d) four-pleasure framework, [4]

Each stage supplies exactly the facts the next part needs. Build 10-mark case studies this way.

### 5.4 SL and HL sharing

Because HL 1, 2, 3, 6, 7 and SL 1, 2, 3, 6, 7 are the same case studies with different parts, you can and should write **one set of case studies and two sets of questions**. This halves the work of producing a paired SL/HL mock and is what the IB itself did.

The two swapped case studies at HL should carry HL-only content. In the specimen these are structural systems (aircraft wing, cantilever, factor of safety, Young's modulus) and materials and structural systems again (airship, composites, strength-to-weight ratio, static and dynamic forces). Other good HL-swap territory: mechanical systems, electronic systems, production systems, life-cycle analysis, design for manufacture strategies, beyond usability.

## 6. The HL Design Thinking Challenge

This is new, it is 30 of 80 marks, and it is HL only. It is the part of Paper 2 that a teacher writing a mock is most likely to get wrong, because there is no precedent for it in any legacy paper.

### 6.1 What it is

A single extended scenario, running from question 8 to question 10, which walks the candidate through a compressed design cycle:

| Q | Heading in the paper | Task | Marks |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 8 | *Design Thinking Challenge* → *Product Analysis* | Analyse the strengths and weaknesses of two existing solutions | 3 + 3 = 6 |
| 9 | *Analyse the challenge* | Construct a problem statement summarizing the analysis and stating design intentions | 2 + 2 + 2 = 6 |
| 10(a) | *The Design Specifications (success criteria for the product)* | Complete a specification table of essential and desirable criteria | 5 |
| 10(b) | | Produce an accurate, labelled, formal annotated diagram of the solution | 5 |
| 10(c) | *Evaluation* | Evaluate the impact of the designed solution | 2 + 3 + 3 = 8 |

### 6.2 The scenario in the specimen

The Loma village rainwater harvesting challenge, in the order the paper presents it:

1. **A humanitarian framing** citing the UN 2030 goal on safe drinking water and Oxfam International. Two or three paragraphs.
2. **Figure 25:** aerial view of the village location.
3. **Figure 26:** flash flooding scenario in the village.
4. **Figure 27: user persona overview.** A four-column table with `User` / `Persona` / `Needs Analysis` / `Interview`, three personas (Nurse, Teacher, Farmer), each with age, role, location, specific quantified needs (a cow needs 30 gallons per day, one persona has a large storage tank, another has none) and a short first-person interview quote.
5. **Product Analysis heading**, then **Figure 28 and Figure 29**, two existing rainwater harvesting systems, each an annotated diagram, with `Strengths:` and `Weaknesses:` answer boxes below each. → Q8
6. **Analyse the challenge heading** and a paragraph restating the problem. → Q9
7. **A restatement of the need**, then **Figure 30**, a partial existing system with a components list including specifications ("100 mm half-round composite guttering").
8. **Figure 31: Design constraints, work force and equipment.** A table: constraints, workforce ("Three labourers for 24 hours"), equipment ("Small earth-moving vehicle", "Basic hand tools (saw, wire cutters, pliers)").
9. **Figure 32: Material properties and classifications.** A two-column shopping list with quantities: "200 × 20 L polyethylene (PE)", "50 metres of 12 mm nylon polypropylene rope", "20 (5 metre) length × width × quantity of bamboo (biomaterial)", "Potable water test kit".
10. **Figure 33: The ACCESS FM analysis framework**, given as a reference table.
11. **10(a):** a partly pre-filled specification table with ACCESS FM letters down the side and Essential / Desirable columns. Some cells are already filled ("A: Empathetic to the Loma village environment"), the candidate completes the rest.
12. **10(b):** the drawing task, with the instruction *"Students may use a pencil and a ruler only (no pen or highlighters) to produce an accurate and labelled formal annotated diagram."*
13. **Figure 34: user persona feedback**, a table where the three personas answer three questions about the completed project, supplying the evidence the candidate must cite. → 10(c)

### 6.3 How to build your own challenge

Follow the same twelve-step spine. The scenario needs to be:

- **Humanitarian or civic in framing**, with a real organization and a real global goal cited. Water, sanitation, energy access, shelter, food storage, healthcare access, disaster response, mobility, education access.
- **Resource-constrained**, so that the specification and drawing tasks have something to bite on. The constraint tables are what make the design task markable.
- **Quantified.** Fifty people. Three labourers for 24 hours. 2000 L tank. 40 metres of piping. A cow needing 30 gallons a day. Without numbers, 10(a) and 10(b) cannot be marked.
- **Supported by three or four personas** with conflicting needs. The nurse wants a small clean supply, the farmer wants a large agricultural supply. That conflict is what makes the specification task non-trivial.
- **Closed by feedback data** (Figure 34) that the evaluation question can require the candidate to cite. This is the mechanism that stops 10(c) becoming free-form opinion.

Non-negotiable: **the questions stay anchored to the scenario throughout.** Question 10(c) is not "Evaluate your design", it is "Evaluate the impact of your rainwater harvesting system design **on the Loma village**", and the markscheme requires "evidence extracted from the interview data".

### 6.4 Timing

HL Paper 2 is 150 minutes for 80 marks, so roughly 1.9 minutes per mark. The challenge is 30 marks, or about 55 minutes. Say so if you are producing guidance for students: the first seven questions should take about 95 minutes.

## 7. Writing the markscheme

Paper 2 markschemes are substantial documents and they are half the value of a mock. Reproduce the structure.

### 7.1 Front matter

1. Cover page: `Markscheme` / `Specimen paper` / `Design technology` / `Higher level` / `Paper 2`.
2. Confidentiality notice (adapt for school use, or drop it).
3. **The annotation table.** The specimen lists the marking annotations available to examiners: tick (awards 1 mark), cross, `0`, `A+` analysis, `B+` balanced argument, `BOD` benefit of the doubt, `NBOD` no benefit of the doubt, `^` caret for omission, `D` description, `EVAL` evaluation, `GEXP` good explanation, `NAQ` not answered question, `OC` off course, `SEEN`, `WTTE` words to that effect, plus vague/repetition/opinion/unclear marks. Worth reproducing in a school mock: it teaches students how their scripts are actually read.

### 7.2 The marking table

Four columns, repeated on every page with the page header:

| Question | Answers | Notes | Total |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |

- **Question:** number and letter in separate sub-columns, `2.` and `c`.
- **Answers:** a bulleted list of creditable points, each ending with a tick symbol. Grouped under short bold headings where the answer has structure (`Strength`, `Weight`, `Psycho pleasure`, `Physio pleasure`, `Laser cutting:`, `Band saw:`).
- **Notes:** the award instruction. See §7.3.
- **Total:** the mark, or a banded expression like `3+3 [6]` or `2+3+3 [8]`.

`Continued` appears bottom right when a question's markscheme runs onto the next page.

### 7.3 Award-instruction formulas

These are near-formulaic. Copy the register.

| Tariff | Formula from the specimen |
| --- | --- |
| [1] | `Award [1] for one relevant point about how user-centered research method may have been used to understand the target person for the yoga organizer up to [1 max].` |
| [1] | `Award [1] for a brief correct answer / name of material property classification up to [1 max].` |
| [2] | `Award [1] for each detail, leading to an account up to [2 max].` |
| [2] | `Award [1] for each relevant detail identified up to [2 max].` |
| [2] | `Award [1] for each detail considered leading to a balanced account of how the wood fibers meet the requirements of design for a circular economy up to [2 max].` |
| [3] | `Award [1] for each relevant review point / evidence considered / balanced reason / cause of how a primary persona may have been used to guide the design development for the list below up to [3 max].` |
| [3] | `Award [1] for each valid reason / evidence to support why: • Cost [1] • Availability [1] • Environmental [1] Influences the selection of materials for the jar opener up to [3 max].` |
| [4] | `Award [1] for each relevant detail / evidence / reason / cause relating to how one of the four-pleasure framework contributes and influences the user experience up to [4 max].` |
| [4] | `Award [1] for each distinct strength / limitation leading to an appraisal of the differences between useability of the early MOM prototype and the later MOM prototype up to [4 max].` |
| [6] | `Award [1] for each relevant strength / weakness of the existing design solutions ... in the context of the Loma Village up to [6 marks].` |

Pattern to reproduce:

```
Award [1] for each <unit of credit> <relating to / leading to> <a restatement of the
question, naming the case study> up to [n max].
```

The `<unit of credit>` slot escalates with the AO: *detail* → *relevant detail* → *reason / cause / evidence* → *distinct strength / limitation leading to an appraisal*. Slash-separated alternatives are the house style and are how the markscheme signals that examiners should accept a range of framings.

### 7.4 Answer content

Three things the specimen markschemes do that a teacher-written one usually does not:

1. **Over-supply creditable points.** A [2] item is given four to eight bullets. A [4] item is given ten or more, grouped. Markers pick two. This is what makes the markscheme usable by someone other than the author.
2. **Quote the syllabus verbatim as the standard.** Bullets frequently open `Students must be able to identify where the 5th, 50th and 5th–95th percentiles are appropriate for a design scenario.` This ties the credit directly back to the guide. Do the same, quoting from the relevant A/B/C sub-topic.
3. **Add a definition bullet where the term is the point.** `A persona is a generalized profile of users who are experiencing a challenge in a process that presents a design opportunity [definition].` The `[definition]` tag tells the marker this bullet is creditable on its own.

Additional guidance sits in the Notes column for the harder items, for example on Q8:

> Consider whether the student's response is examining the strengths and weaknesses [do not a mark simply for positives and negatives of the system alone].

and a list of the strands to look for, headed `Design in context [AO3]` and `Design in practice [AO3]`, with the syllabus areas beneath.

### 7.5 Banded extended responses

For the 6- and 8-mark challenge items the markscheme uses a two-column band table instead of a bullet list.

Question 9, `Construct a problem statement` [6], marked `2+2+2`:

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Design Intentions.** Summarize how/why the suggested outcome would solve the challenge **(2)** | Full, partial or incomplete success, making a judgment against the specifications **(2)** |
| **Describe the need.** Summarize the challenge **(2)** | Full, partial or incomplete success against the Design Intentions **(2)** |
| **Summary of a reflective and considered thinking approach.** Summarize the impact of the proposed solution on the Village **(2)** | Full, partial or incomplete success, making a judgment on the overall problem solving of the design thinking challenge **(2)** |

Question 10(c), `Evaluate` [8], marked `2+3+3`:

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **Design Intentions.** Describe how/why the designed outcome meets the design specifications. *Has access to fresh water improved?* | Full, partial or incomplete success, making a judgment against the specifications **(2)** |
| **Meets the need.** Evaluate how the design outcome meets the needs of the Loma village by providing evidence extracted from the interview data. *What are the benefits of the water harvesting project?* | Full, partial or incomplete success against the Design Intentions **(3)** |
| **Extent of function/feasibility.** Demonstrate the impact of the proposed solution on the village. *Does flooding impact the freshwater supply?* | Full, partial or incomplete success, making a judgment on the overall problem solving of the design thinking challenge **(3)** |

Note the italicized questions in the left column. They are lifted from the persona feedback table (Figure 34), which is how the paper forces the candidate to use the supplied data.

Beneath each band table the specimen gives `Sample answers` as bullets, prefaced with `Limitations/challenges include (but are not limited to):`.

### 7.6 Marking a drawing

Question 10(b) is the only drawing item. Its markscheme has three parts:

**Band table:**

| | |
| --- | --- |
| Design in theory | Full, partial or incomplete success against the Design intentions **(2)** |
| Design in context | Full, partial or incomplete success against the Design Specification **(2)** |
| Design in practice | Full, partial or incomplete annotations **(1)** |

**`What to look out for:`** prose guidance, including the unusually blunt instruction:

> Consider if the student has analysed and input ALL of the details they have been offered, if they have missed any elements take 1 mark away for each missing element.

and prompts on clarity, whether a logical design process is evident, and whether enough detail is present for the solution to be built.

**Two exemplar drawings**, labelled `Example 1` and `Example 2`.

If you write a design item, you must supply exemplar drawings. Without them the item is not markable by anyone but you.

## 10. Build order

1. **Draw the coverage grid.** The AO split follows from the tariff ladder, so decide coverage rather than AO percentages (§4.4).
2. **Choose five shared case studies** (6, 6, 6, 10, 10 marks) plus **two HL-swap case studies** if you are producing a paired paper.
3. **Choose one of the shared cases to be IA-derived**, using anonymized student work.
4. **Write the case study text, tables and figures.** Fact-load them (see [paper1.md §4.3](paper1.md)).
5. **Write each question part on the tariff ladder**, `[1] [2] [3]` for a 6-mark case and `[2] [2] [2] [4]` for a 10-mark case. Choose the command term to fit the tariff (§4.1), not the other way round.
6. **Run the deletion test** on every part (§5.1).
7. **HL only: build the Design Thinking Challenge** to the twelve-step spine in §6.2. Write the persona table, the constraints table, the resources table and the feedback table before you write the questions.
8. **Write the markscheme**, over-supplying creditable points and using the award formulas in §7.3.
9. **Produce exemplar drawings** for any `Design` item.
10. **Set the paper** with answer boxes at two lines per mark, continuation lines, and back-matter references.
11. **Run the checklist.**

## 12. Worked example: a 6-mark case study built from scratch

**Target:** SL, 6 marks, sub-topics C2.2 (circular economy), A3.1 (material properties), C1.2 (inclusive design). Tariff ladder [1][2][3].

*The company is real; the reusable envelope and its twenty-trip service life are invented for this illustration. A live mock should either use verified facts or an invented company, as the specimen does with TrekDesign and Giggle Greetings.*

**Case study text**

> **4.** Vinted is an online marketplace where users buy and sell second-hand clothing directly from each other. The platform handles listing, payment and shipping, and takes no fee from the seller.
>
> Sellers photograph a garment, list it with a description of its size and condition, and post it in packaging of their own choosing when it sells. In 2023 the company introduced pre-paid, reusable shipping envelopes made from recycled polyester, designed to survive at least twenty round trips before recycling.
>
> **Figure 6: Vinted listing interface**
> **Figure 7: Reusable shipping envelope, showing the tear-strip closure and return label**

**Questions**

> **(a)** State one physical property of recycled polyester that makes it suitable for the reusable shipping envelope, see Figure 7. **[1]**
>
> **(b)** Describe how the reusable envelope supports a circular economy approach to packaging, see Figure 7. **[2]**
>
> **(c)** Analyse the accessibility of the Vinted listing process for a seller with limited fine motor control, see Figure 6 and Figure 7. **[3]**

**Markscheme**

| Q | Answers | Notes | Total |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 4. a | • Tear resistance ✓<br>• Low density / lightweight ✓<br>• Water resistance / low absorbency ✓<br>• Durability under repeated flexing ✓ | Award [1] for one relevant physical property of recycled polyester relevant to the reusable envelope up to [1 max]. | 1 |
| 4. b | The envelope: <br>• is designed for reuse rather than single use, keeping the material in circulation ✓<br>• has a stated service life of at least twenty round trips before it leaves use ✓<br>• is made from recycled feedstock, closing the loop on existing polyester ✓<br>• is recycled at end of life rather than landfilled ✓<br>• reduces the demand for virgin packaging material per transaction ✓ | Award [1] for each detail considered leading to a balanced account of how the reusable envelope meets the requirements of design for a circular economy up to [2 max]. | 2 |
| 4. c | Inclusive design ensures products address the needs of the widest possible audience, regardless of age or ability.<br>Listing interface:<br>• Photography requires steady handling of a phone ✓<br>• Small touch targets increase error rate for users with tremor ✓<br>• Text entry for the description is slow without voice input ✓<br>Envelope:<br>• The tear-strip closure needs a pinch grip and sustained pulling force ✓<br>• An adhesive seal would require less grip strength than a tear strip ✓<br>• The pre-printed return label removes a handwriting task, improving accessibility ✓ | Award [1] for each distinct guiding element / structure relating to the accessibility of the Vinted listing process and the envelope for a seller with limited fine motor control up to [3 max]. ## 13. Common failures in teacher-written mocks

| --- | --- |

## 14. Known differences from the legacy paper

| Legacy Paper 2 (pre-2027) | 2027 Paper 2 |
| --- | --- |
| Section A (compulsory) and Section B (choice of one essay) | No sections, no choice, answer all |
| Nested sub-parts `(a)(i)`, `(a)(ii)` | Flat `(a)` `(b)` `(c)` `(d)` |
| SL and HL sat the same paper (50 marks, 1 h 30) | Separate papers: SL 50 marks in 1 h 30, HL 80 marks in 2 h 30 |
| Command terms including `Define`, `Discuss`, `Suggest` | Restricted 15-term glossary |
| Extended-response essay questions of 9 marks in Section B | No essay. The extended work is the HL Design Thinking Challenge |
| No design or drawing task | A 5-mark annotated drawing task at HL |
| Old topic numbering | A/B/C strand codes |
| HL sat an additional Paper 3 | No Paper 3 |

Legacy papers remain useful for harvesting case study subjects and calibrating difficulty. Their structure is obsolete.
