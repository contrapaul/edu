# Design Technology Extended Essay: plan

A student-facing resource page for writing a Design Technology extended essay, built to the first assessment 2027 guide. Lives at `curriculum/dp/ee/index.html`, served at `/curriculum/dp/ee/`, linked from the "More" pills on `curriculum/dp/index.html` (replaces "Placeholder 4").

## Scope

This is a guide to the **design technology** EE, not a guide to the EE. The school's EE coordinator, the student's supervisor and the IB guide own the general process (sessions, RPF, deadlines, formatting, integrity). The page covers what is different, harder or unclear when the subject is design, and points elsewhere for the rest. Where general EE facts are needed for the DT advice to make sense (word count, criteria), they are quoted from the guide with a page reference, not re-explained.

Mr. K is not the school's EE authority and the page says so. DT EEs are rare, and the standing advice students hear is "don't". The page treats a design EE as a perfectly acceptable choice, both subject-focused and as one half of an interdisciplinary essay (IB's own interdisciplinary examples pair design technology with language and literature and with digital society, guide pp. 21 to 22). It does not oversell: the essay is hard, the pitfalls are specific, and the page names them.

Voice: two kinds of content only. "From the guide" boxes quote IB with a page reference. "Mr. K notes" boxes hold everything else: suggestions, interpretation, opinion. One label, since none of this is a tested method yet.

The page is for students anywhere. Nothing depends on our school's calendar or systems.

## 2. What the page is for

IB's DT-specific advice is short (six pages) and assumes the reader has already absorbed the generic guidance, the sciences group guidance and the criteria. The DT-specific questions a student actually has are answered nowhere in one place:

- Is my topic design, or is it economics, psychology, business or history in disguise?
- Do I have to build something?
- Can I use my IA product, research or models?
- What does "use the design thinking process" mean in an essay with no client and no final product?
- Which DT research tools count as research methods, and how do I justify them?
- Where do photographs, drawings, test data and prototypes go, and do they count toward 4,000 words?
- What does a DT essay look like at criterion C and D, where descriptive essays fail?

The page answers those, in that order, with the relevant IB text quoted beside each answer.

## 4. Page outline

Hero: title, breadcrumb (Curriculum / DP / Extended Essay), a one-line "how to read this" naming the two voices, and one line saying the school's EE coordinator and the IB guide are the authority on the process.

Sticky table of contents on the left as in the IA guide. Sections collapsible.

### 4.1 A design EE is a good choice

- Why students are told "don't", and why the page disagrees. Mr. K notes.
- What IB says a DT EE is: design thinking as the central methodology, structuring "the inquiry and analysis of problems, the development of feasible solutions, and the testing and evaluation of the selected solutions". Subject-specific guidance, opening paragraph, quoted.
- What is the same as every other EE: 4,000 words, 500-word reflective statement, about 40 hours, three reflection sessions, criteria A to E out of 30, external assessment. Guide pp. 13 to 14. One table, then a pointer to the coordinator and guide for process detail.
- How it counts (decision 3.2).
- **EE versus the IA design project.** Table: purpose, output, limit, assessment, what design thinking does in each. The overlap rule quoted (guide p. 36) and the DT inappropriate-topic bullet on overlap. Mr. K notes: an IA-shaped essay fails on C and D because it presents a product instead of answering a question.
- Design as an interdisciplinary partner (decision 3.4).

### 4.2 Is my topic design?

- IB's three context starters (artefacts that fail, last badly or waste resources; needs of particular user groups; new technologies converging with existing ones). Subject-specific guidance, "Choosing a topic".
- The four inappropriate topics, quoted.
- The "mobile devices" example from the guidance: same topic, three lenses, only one is DT. Paraphrased with reference.
- **The design lens test.** Mr. K notes. Five questions: Does answering it require analysing how something is designed, made or used? Could a student of another subject write it without changing the question? Is there a design solution or decision that can be evaluated? Is there primary data I can collect myself? Do I have the materials, equipment and access to model or test in enough detail? (The last is from the DT note on criterion A.)
- Sciences group cautions: realistic about facilities, no university-lab topics, no topics where data collection is unethical or impractical, "do not overcomplicate it". Guide p. 64.
- **IB's six examples** in a table (topic, RQ, approach), tagged by research type. Subject-specific guidance and guide p. 66.
- **Topic areas.** Mr. K notes, draft for Paul to edit: what tends to work, what rarely works. Same list as presentation slide 3.

### 4.3 Writing the question

- Must be a question, not a hypothesis or statement of intent. Guide p. 84.
- Clear, focused, arguable. Guide p. 86 (figure 14), rebuilt as three cards.
- Higher-order wording, not double-barrelled, not self-evident. Guide pp. 38 to 39.
- **RQ anatomy for design.** Mr. K notes. A workable design RQ names a product or system, a property or user need, a user group or context, and a bounded place or scale. IB's six examples annotated against this.
- **Rate this research question.** Interactive, using the rewritten set from `presentation.md`.
- Changing the question is normal. Guide p. 86.

### 4.4 Research methods for a design essay

- Secondary research (literature review) is required in every EE. Guide p. 38. DT: both primary and secondary, or literature only "provided that you manipulate or analyse the data in an original and creative way". Quoted.
- Practical work is optional and must be justified. The five forms, quoted.
- Locally available primary sources encouraged. Quoted.
- **Methods table.** Mr. K notes. Interviews, questionnaires, observation and task analysis, focus groups, user testing, materials and product testing, modelling and prototyping, life-cycle analysis, triple bottom line, finite element analysis, literature review. For each: what it produces, which design-thinking stage it serves, what it costs in time and access. The DT note on criterion C names these.
- Approaches considered and rejected must be included. Subject-specific guidance. Mr. K notes on how to write that in two paragraphs.
- Sciences rules that still bind: replicable method, controlled variables for primary data, SI units, uncertainties, stated selection criteria for secondary data. Guide pp. 67 to 68.
- Ethics for the methods a design student is most likely to use: consent and comfort of interview and test participants, sciences experimentation guidelines for materials testing. Guide p. 96, pointer to the IB ethical guidelines. Nothing more on integrity here; that is the coordinator's.

### 4.5 Writing it so the criteria can see it

- The mandatory structure and the word count table, quoted. Guide pp. 87 to 88. Photographs, drawings, tables, charts and calculations are outside the count.
- Examiners are not required to read appendices or footnotes. Guide p. 89. For a design essay this matters most: test data, prototype photographs and sketches go in the body.
- **A design essay structure.** Mr. K notes. Body sections mapped to design thinking: context and the design problem; existing solutions and literature; methods and justification with rejected approaches; findings from user research, testing and modelling; analysis and evaluation of design solutions; unexpected outcomes; conclusion tied to the RQ; unresolved issues. Word budget adding to 4,000.
- Line of argument: the thread linking RQ, findings and conclusions. Guide pp. 40 to 41.
- Evaluation is integrated throughout, not a section. Guide p. 41 and DT note on D. Unexpected outcomes are reported "even if they were not part of the original plan". Negative results are valid. Guide p. 67.
- Conclusion synthesizes, names unresolved issues, suggests further investigation. DT note on D.

### 4.6 The criteria, as they apply to design

For each of A to E: guiding question and level descriptor table quoted (pp. 112 to 114), then the DT note and the sciences note quoted, then one Mr. K notes box on what that looks like in a design essay. Marks: A 6, B 6, C 6, D 8, E 4. D is the largest and is where descriptive essays fall down.

Criterion E is applied only to the reflective statement (guide p. 112). One Mr. K notes box on what a design student has to reflect on (a changed question, a failed test, a rejected method) and a pointer to the guide's reflection section for the process.

Grade descriptors collapsed (pp. 115 to 117).

### 4.7 Before you submit

Short checklist, DT items only: no IA overlap; evidence in the body not appendices; figures labelled and referred to; methods justified and rejected approaches named; units and uncertainties; participant consent recorded. General submission checks (anonymity, file size, RPF) in one line pointing to the coordinator.

## 5. Interactive elements

Plain JS in `ee.js`, no build step.

1. **Rate this research question** (4.3). Card per RQ, clear/focused/arguable/design-lens toggles, reveal a judgement.
2. **Word budget** (4.5). Section targets against 4,000, with a reminder of what is outside the count.
3. **Criterion self-check** (4.6). Pick the descriptor per strand, get a best-fit estimate and the weakest strands. Labelled as a self-estimate.

Copy-to-clipboard frames: RQ anatomy, methods justification, rejected-approaches paragraph.
