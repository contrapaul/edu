# Design Technology Extended Essay: student briefing

About ten minutes, seven slides, one activity. Audience: DP students who are exploring an EE and might choose design. Not a process briefing; the coordinator does that. This is about whether design is for them and what it takes.

On-slide text is what goes on the slide. Speaker notes are yours. Every factual claim is traced in the sources table at the end.

**On the slide**

> A Design Technology Extended Essay

> Ten minutes on whether it is for you

**Speaker notes**

I am not the EE coordinator and I am not going to talk about deadlines, forms or sessions. I supervise design essays. This is about what a design essay is, what it is not, and how to tell if you have one.

## Slide 2. You may have been told not to

**On the slide**

> Design essays are rare. That makes them unfamiliar, not a bad idea.
>
> IB lists design technology as an EE subject, publishes guidance for it, and uses it in its own examples of interdisciplinary essays.
>
> The real risk is different: students try to write a second design project. A design essay answers a question.

**Speaker notes**

The standard advice students hear is "don't". Three reasons it gets said: there are few examples to copy, supervisors outside the subject are unsure how to help, and students who try it often produce an IA in essay form. None of those is a reason to avoid it if you have a real design question and access to what you need to investigate it.

The failure mode is specific and avoidable. The IA gives you a persona and asks for a product. The EE gives you a question and asks for evidence and an argument. Same thinking process, different output. Every slide after this one is about staying on the essay side of that line.

Design can also be one half of an interdisciplinary essay. IB's own examples pair it with language and literature and with digital society. If your idea sits between design and another subject you take, that pathway exists; talk to the coordinator about it.

## Slide 3. Five essentials for a design essay

**On the slide**

> 01  It answers a question with evidence. You investigate a design problem, develop or test solutions and evaluate them. You are not delivering a product.
>
> 02  The question is about how something is designed, made or used. If an economics, psychology or history student could write it without changing the wording, it is not design.
>
> 03  You need published sources plus data of your own: user testing, interviews, observation, materials tests, models. Sources alone are allowed only if your analysis of them is original.
>
> 04  Building or testing something is optional. If you do, say why. If you do not, say why. Small and justified beats big and unexplained.
>
> 05  Nothing in it can come from your IA: not the product, the research, the models or the sources.
>
> 4,000 words · about 40 hours · the same five criteria as every other subject

**Speaker notes**

01. IB's words are that design thinking should structure "the inquiry and analysis of problems, the development of feasible solutions, and the testing and evaluation of the selected solutions". That is the shape of the essay. Problem, solutions, evaluation, in writing, with evidence.

02. IB gives the example of mobile phones. Technological, social and economic angles all exist. Only the technological one, the design and development of the device, is a design essay. The lifestyle and health effects belong to other subjects. Ask yourself who else could write your question unchanged.

03. A literature review is required in every EE in every subject. Design normally expects primary data on top. A literature-only design essay is permitted, but the guidance says you must "manipulate or analyse the data in an original and creative way", which is harder than it sounds. IB also encourages "locally available primary sources". Your school, your city, the people around you.

04. IB lists what practical work can look like: experiments on materials or products, modelling, prototyping, full realization and trialling. It also says practical work "is not compulsory and must be justified". Forty hours is enough for a small test rig or a series of models. It is not enough for a finished product plus an essay about it.

05. The guide makes it your responsibility, with your supervisor, that the essay does not duplicate other work submitted for the diploma. The design guidance goes further and names an essay that "overlaps with the design project and uses the same literature, experiments, models or resources" as an inappropriate topic. Different product, different sources, different models.

Footer: the criteria are generic. A is the framework (question, methods, structure), B knowledge, C analysis and argument, D discussion and evaluation, E reflection. D is worth the most. A descriptive essay scores low on C and D regardless of subject.

## Slide 4. Topic areas

**On the slide**

> Tends to work
>
> 01  One product, one user group, tested: the ergonomics of a product for people with a specific need
> 02  One component, one material swap, one property you can measure
> 03  Two designs of the same packaging or product compared over their life cycle
> 04  Assistive or inclusive design for a need you can observe in a real person
> 05  Two ways of making the same part, prototyped and compared on strength, cost or time
>
> Rarely works
> The history of a product. The future of a technology. Whether a whole industry can become sustainable. A review of a product you like. Anything you cannot observe, test or measure yourself.

**Speaker notes**

The working list is mine, drawn from the kinds of question IB uses in its own examples: an aquaponics system for small farmers in one city, a study bedroom converted for a wheelchair user, an ergonomic and sustainability evaluation of one water bottle, egg packaging for retailers in one country. Every one names a product or system, a user or context, and a place. That pattern is the point, more than the specific topics.

The "rarely works" list is IB's list of inappropriate topics in my words: purely historical, a review of a product or development, a psychological or marketing angle, plus the sciences group's warning against topics that need equipment you do not have or data you cannot ethically collect.

(Edit both lists from your own supervising experience before presenting.)

## Slide 5. Rate the question

**On the slide**

> For each: is it clear? focused? arguable? design?
>
> A  How likely is it that electric scooters will replace bicycles for short trips in Shanghai by 2035?
>
> B  How have running shoes changed since 1970?
>
> C  To what extent does the handle of the [brand] electric kettle meet the needs of users with reduced grip strength?
>
> D  Can 3D printing improve furniture making?
>
> E  Which of three compostable materials is most suitable for takeaway containers used by street vendors in Chengdu, judged on strength, cost and end of life?
>
> F  Is the [popular phone] well designed?

**Speaker notes**

IB's test for a research question is three words: clear, focused, arguable. Will the reader understand what you are investigating? Is it narrow enough for 4,000 words? Does it allow analysis and a reasoned argument rather than a description or an opinion? I add a fourth: is it design?

Give them two minutes, then:

A. Clear, but not design and not arguable with design evidence. It predicts adoption, which is a transport policy or economics question. No design decision gets evaluated. Not fixable without becoming a different question.

B. Clear, but purely historical and descriptive. IB names this type as inappropriate. A student would produce a timeline. Could be rescued by narrowing to one design change and one measurable effect, but the student who wrote it wanted to write about history.

C. Clear, focused, arguable, design. A product, a user need, a property. It needs access to users with reduced grip strength and a method (task analysis, user trials, grip measurement). Small, testable, and the evaluation writes itself. This is the shape to copy.

D. Yes or no, no scale, no context. "Furniture making" is an industry. It would produce a general survey of 3D printing. Fixable: for one joint in one flat-pack chair, compare a printed connector with a CNC-cut plywood one on strength, cost and assembly time. Now it is C-shaped.

E. Design, testable, bounded to one place and one use. Three materials and three criteria is a lot for 4,000 words but manageable if the tests are simple. Watch the word count. Good.

F. Vague, invites opinion, and is a product review, which IB names as inappropriate. No user group, no property, no comparison. What would count as evidence? Nothing the student could collect.

Ask what C and E have that the others lack: a product or system, a user or use, a place, and a property that can be measured or judged with evidence. That is the anatomy of a design question.

## Slide 6. Do and do not

**On the slide**

> ✓ Do
>
> Choose something you can get your hands on: the product, the users and the workshop or lab.
>
> Put the product, the user, the place and the property you will judge into the question itself.
>
> Use the subject's tools and name them: task analysis, user trials, life-cycle analysis, materials testing, prototyping. Say what you rejected and why.
>
> ✕ Do not
>
> Recycle your IA. Different product, different sources, different models.
>
> Describe. A page on how a product evolved earns nothing at criteria C or D.
>
> Hide evidence in appendices. Examiners are not required to read them. Photographs, tables, drawings and data in the body are outside the word count.

**Speaker notes**

First do: the design guidance says a question is unsuitable "if the equipment or materials necessary to construct or model the design in sufficient detail for evaluation are not available to you". Access is a requirement, not a nice-to-have.

Second do: the anatomy from the last slide.

Third do: IB's list of tools for design analysis includes observation, interviews, questionnaires, focus groups, testing and task analysis, alongside life-cycle analysis, triple bottom line and finite element analysis. The guidance also asks you to "include reasons why any approaches to the design problem were considered and rejected". That paragraph is easy to write and most students forget it.

First don't: the overlap rule again, because it is the one that gets essays disqualified.

Second don't: criterion C's lowest band is "the essay is descriptive rather than analytical". Criterion D wants a balanced discussion of the significance of your findings, supported by evidence. Evolution-of-the-product writing satisfies neither.

Third don't: the guide says anything important to the line of argument must not be in appendices, footnotes or endnotes. For a design essay, evidence is visual and numerical, and the word count excludes charts, diagrams, annotated illustrations, tables and calculations. Put it in the body, label it, and refer to it in the text.
