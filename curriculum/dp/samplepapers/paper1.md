# Paper 1 Style Guide and Question-Writing Instructions

**DP Design Technology, first examinations 2027**

This document tells a teacher or an AI how to write a mock Paper 1 that looks and behaves like the real thing. Follow it literally. Where it states a rule, the rule is derived from evidence, and the evidence is named.

## 3. The one rule that matters most: everything is a case study

**This is the defining feature of the new Paper 1 and the easiest thing to get wrong.**

Paper 1 is not a bank of context-free recall questions. In the 2027 specimen there is **not a single item that stands free of a stimulus**. Every question is either attached to a named product, company, project or artefact, or it is a sub-part of a numbered case study that carries several items.

Read the pattern:

1. **An opening run of single-mark items**, several of which share one running context (TrekDesign). These are the lightest-weight items but they are still contextualized. Even Q1, the closest thing to a free-standing item, hangs off a figure.
2. **A middle of five- and three-part case studies.**
3. **A long closing case study of nine parts** on a single high-profile product, in both papers.

Reproduce this shape. A mock Paper 1 that opens with "Which of the following is a thermoplastic?" is the wrong paper.

### What "focused on the case study" means in practice

Every stem must be answerable only by someone holding the case study in mind. The specimen achieves this in three ways, and you should use all three.

**(a) Name the subject inside the stem.** Not "Which gear system optimizes torque and speed?" but:

> In the design of the Sphero BOLT robot (Figure 10), which type of gear system would most effectively optimize torque and speed for its movement capabilities?

**(b) Point at the figure explicitly.** The specimen uses `see Figure 10`, `(Figure 10)`, `see Figure 13 and Figure 14`, `(see Figures 16–18)`. Almost every case-study item carries one of these.

**(c) Make the correct option depend on the case facts.** This is the real test. If you can delete the case study and the question still has one right answer, the question is not anchored, it is decorated. Compare:

- *Decorated:* "Which pleasure category is associated with aesthetic appeal? A. Physio B. Socio C. Ideo D. Psycho." True regardless of context.
- *Anchored (specimen 9a):* "Which pleasure category of the four-pleasure framework is most closely associated with **the aesthetic appeal of gold jewellery**?" The candidate must apply the framework to *this* product.
- *Strongly anchored (specimen 13h):* "To ensure optimal performance and efficiency of the electric powertrain and battery system, Tesla's Model 3 employs a complex electronic control unit (ECU). **Identify the non-essential function of this ECU.**" Requires reading the case, the stem, and the syllabus content together.

Aim for at least two thirds of your items to be anchored or strongly anchored. Decorated items are acceptable only in the opening single-mark run, and even there the specimen keeps a named company attached.

## 4. Writing the case studies

Case studies are the hard part of Paper 1 and are worth treating as a separate task, done first. Write all your case studies before you write a single item.

### 4.1 What the specimen draws on

| Category | Specimen examples |
| --- | --- |
| Named consumer product, recent | Sphero BOLT, Terra AI Compass, Tesla Model 3, Shift Robotics Moonwalkers, Arc'teryx MO/GO |
| Design-classic with a documented sustainability story | Herman Miller Mirra chair |
| Architecture and construction | BIG Dortheavej affordable housing, Romses prefab prototype |
| A materials or process story | 3D printed jewellery and gold hallmarking, mycelium packaging |
| An invented but plausible company | TrekDesign, FUN Gi's, Giggle Greetings |
| Infographics and process diagrams | Circular economy diagram, e-textile construction diagram |
| **Real DP student IA work** | "Physical prototype testing of a mycelium food container from a May 2024 DP Design Technology student project", with the student's own annotations and survey charts |

That last row is worth noting. The IB is willing to put anonymized student project artefacts, including annotated CAD, prototype photographs and survey result charts, straight into Paper 1. Mock papers should do the same. It is authentic, it is free of copyright difficulty, and it signals to students that IA skills are examined.

### 4.2 Case study anatomy

A case study in the specimen has, in order:

1. **A number and a one- to four-sentence framing paragraph.** Present tense or simple past. Names the product, the company and what it does.
2. **One or more numbered figures** with a caption in the form `Figure 7: Infographic of TrekDesign woven fleece`. Captions are descriptive, sentence case after the colon, no terminal full stop.
3. **Optional extra body text** giving the facts the items will hinge on: materials, percentages, costs, dimensions, market position, manufacturing method, user population.
4. **Lettered parts (a), (b), (c)...** each a complete MCQ.

Longer case studies interleave further figures and text between parts. Question 9 in the HL specimen introduces Figure 8 at part (c) and Figure 9 at part (d), each with a short block of new text. This is a good technique: it lets a nine-part case study introduce new content without a wall of reading at the top.

### 4.3 Rules for writing a good one

- **Load the case with facts that items will use.** The Mirra chair paragraph supplies "42% recycled materials", "96% of its weight is recyclable", "taken apart in 15 minutes", and a five-criterion environmental rating tool. Every one of those is a hook an item could hang on. Aim for roughly two usable facts per intended item.
- **Cover more than one syllabus theme per case.** The Tesla case reaches A1 (psychology of purchase), B1.1 (UCD and cognitive load), A3.2 (static forces), A3.3 (mechanical systems), C3 (IoT and data), B4.1 (robotics in production), A3.1 (Young's modulus), A3.4 (ECU functions) and C4.1 (DFM). This is what lets one stimulus carry nine items. If your case only supports material properties questions, it is too narrow.
- **Make the product real and checkable.** Use a product a student could look up. The one exception is the invented-company device, used when the IB wants a generic scenario (TrekDesign, FUN Gi's, Giggle Greetings). Invented companies get a plausible sector and a plausible design brief, nothing more. **We narrow this further than the specimen does: see §4.5.**
- **Keep the reading load honest.** The longest single case study body in the HL specimen (Q13, Tesla) is about 130 words before the items start. Q11 is about 110. Q8 is about 100. Nothing exceeds roughly 150 words. Paper 1 is 90 minutes for 40 items at HL, which is about 2¼ minutes per item including all reading.
- **Write globally.** The specimen ranges across Denmark, Indonesia, the US and unnamed developing-world contexts, uses both metric and imperial with metric first ("358 miles (576 km)"), and avoids culture-specific idiom.
- **Attribute images.** Live papers carry a full reference list at the back in Harvard-ish form with `[Accessed 14 October 2024]` and `source adapted.` where the image was modified. A school mock should carry the same list. It models academic honesty and it makes the paper reusable.

### 4.4 Distribution of case studies across a paper

For a **40-mark HL paper**, a workable allocation:

- 7 single-mark items, of which 5 or 6 share one running context
- 3 case studies of 5 items
- 1 case study of 3 items
- 1 case study of 6 items
- 1 case study of 9 items

That is 7 + 15 + 3 + 6 + 9 = 40, which is the HL specimen's own distribution.

For a **30-mark SL paper**:

- 5 single-mark items sharing one or two contexts
- 2 case studies of 5 items
- 2 case studies of 3 items
- 1 case study of 9 items

That is 5 + 10 + 6 + 9 = 30, which is the SL specimen's own distribution.

### 4.5 Choosing a subject

§4.3 says what makes a case study good once you have the subject. This section says how to pick the subject in the first place. These are house rules, not inferences from the specimen, and where they depart from the IB's own practice that is said plainly.

**Reuse is allowed, on one condition.** The same product may appear in Paper 1 and Paper 2, in SL and HL, and in more than one set, provided each appearance asks about **different sub-topics**. A product reused to ask the same thing twice is a repeat; a product reused to ask about materials in one paper and production systems in another is a second use of a good subject. Record what each appearance covered so the next writer can see what is left. The specimen itself reuses TrekDesign across seven items and the Giggle Greetings and MOM cases across both levels.

**Prefer products with a long service life over products that are merely current.** The IB specimen leans on the recent: Sphero BOLT, Terra AI Compass, Shift Robotics Moonwalkers. Those will be discontinued or renamed long before the paper stops being used, and a candidate who cannot look the product up loses the anchoring the case depends on. Favour subjects that have been in production for years or decades, or that have an established place in design history.

**The exception is a fad, used deliberately.** A product that rose and fell quickly is a weak subject for materials or systems questions and an excellent one for obsolescence, product analysis and the four-pleasure framework. Labubu, the Zune and the Kin phones are worth more as case studies of failure and style obsolescence than most durable products are. Choose a fad on purpose, for the sub-topic that needs it, not by accident because it was in the news.

**Avoid invented companies except where the premise is itself hypothetical.** The specimen uses TrekDesign, FUN Gi's and Giggle Greetings freely; we do not. A real product is checkable, carries verifiable facts, and rewards a candidate's own knowledge. Invent a company only when the question is about method or commercial reasoning rather than about an artefact: a SWOT analysis, a market-entry decision, a production-system choice, a design brief. In those cases the company is a frame for the method and inventing it avoids making false claims about a real business.

**Naming a real company critically is legitimate and often necessary.** C1.1 is about the responsibility of the designer, and that topic cannot be taught honestly on invented examples. Cartridge lockouts, sealed batteries, controller drift and disposable vapes are real design decisions by named manufacturers with real consequences. Two constraints apply: every critical claim must be factually verifiable and stated as what the product does rather than as an accusation of motive, and the case must give the manufacturer's position where one exists, so the candidate weighs an argument rather than receiving a verdict.

**An HL-associated product may anchor an SL case.** The absolute rule is about **content, not subject**: an SL paper may not contain a single mark drawn from HL-only sub-topics. The product carrying the case is unconstrained. A turbine, a gearbox or a games console can appear in an SL paper provided the questions stay inside SL material. This is how a subject bank serves both levels, and §5.4 of [paper2.md](paper2.md) depends on it.

**Keep the 150-word ceiling.** It is measured from the specimen and it holds regardless of subject. A conceptually demanding product gets a shorter body and more of its facts carried in a table or figure, not a longer paragraph.

#### The screening test

Before a subject enters the bank, it must pass all five:

1. **Breadth.** It supports items across at least three syllabus sub-topics (§4.3). This rules out more candidates than anything else.
2. **Checkability.** A student can look it up and the facts can be verified.
3. **Longevity, or deliberate fad.** It will still be recognisable in five years, or it is being used precisely because it was not.
4. **Fact density.** Roughly two usable facts per intended item are available or findable.
5. **Sector.** It adds to the spread rather than deepening a cluster the paper already has (§4.1, and [paper2.md §13](paper2.md)).

### 4.6 Subject bank

Candidates that pass the §4.5 screening test, grouped by sector so that a paper can be built with the spread §4.1 requires. **Sub-topics** lists what the subject can reach, not what it must be used for. **Status** records where it has already appeared, so §4.5's reuse rule can be applied.

This is a working bank. Add to it whenever a subject survives the screening test, and record each use.

## 5. Item anatomy and style rules

### 5.1 Stems

Observed stem openings, with their frequency of use in the specimen roughly in descending order:

- `Which of the following ...` / `Which one activity best describes ...` / `Which best describes ...`
- `Which [noun] is most critical / most effectively / most closely associated with ...`
- `What is the correct term for ...` / `What is the main disadvantage of ...` / `What role do ... play in ...`
- `How does ... contribute to / enhance / align with ...`
- `Identify ...` / `Determine why ...` / `Select the missing elements`
- `In the context of ..., which ...`

Rules:

1. **The stem is a complete question ending in a question mark**, or (rarely) an instruction ending in a full stop: `Figure 1 shows the elements of a circular economy process, select the missing elements.` Prefer the question form.
2. **Superlatives do the discriminating work.** `best`, `most critical`, `most effectively`, `main` appear constantly. They signal that more than one option may be partly defensible and the candidate must rank. Use them for AO2 and AO3 items; drop them for AO1 recall items where exactly one option is correct.
3. **Name the product and the figure.** See §3.
4. **Do not negate without flagging.** The specimen uses `main disadvantage` and `non-essential function` rather than "which is NOT". If you must negate, put the negative word in the stem in a form that cannot be skim-read past.
5. **Length.** One sentence typically, two at most. Where more setup is needed, put it in a paragraph above the stem, as the specimen does for 9(c) and 13(h).
6. **Introduce a framework by its full name and then its abbreviation**, once: `user-centred design (UCD)`, `design for manufacture (DFM)`, `Attract, Converse, and Transact (ACT) model`, `finite element analysis (FEA)`, `computer-aided design (CAD)`, `Internet of Things (IoT)`.

### 5.2 Options

Formatting, exactly as the specimen sets it:

```
A.   Tensile strength

B.   Compressive strength

C.   Toughness

D.   Elasticity
```

- Four options, always. Labelled `A.` `B.` `C.` `D.` with a full stop after the letter.
- **No terminal punctuation** on any option, even full-sentence options.
- Each option starts with a **capital letter**.
- Options are blank-line separated in the printed paper.
- Never use "All of the above", "None of the above", "Both A and B". The specimen never does.

Content rules:

7. **Options are parallel in grammar and comparable in length.** All noun phrases, or all clauses beginning with the same verb form. Where the specimen uses long clause options (12d, 13a–13i) all four are long.
8. **The correct option must not be the longest.** Check every item for this. In specimen 12d the correct option "A rugged, simplified outer casing that can be easily injection moulded" is mid-length among four long options.
9. **Distractors must be plausible to a student who half-knows the content.** The best specimen distractors are (i) the adjacent syllabus term (Compressive vs Tensile strength; SLS vs FDM vs stereolithography), (ii) the right idea applied to the wrong actor (13e offers four genuine IoT benefits, only one of which is a *competitive advantage*), and (iii) the true-but-irrelevant statement.
10. **Avoid distractors that are simply false in an obvious way.** "It has no impact on the mechanical systems" (13d, option D) is weak. One such throwaway per item is tolerable; two makes the item a two-way guess.

### 5.3 The multi-statement item type

Used sparingly and worth including once or twice per paper. Format from specimen 9(b) and 9(e):

```
(e)  What are the main advantages for jewellery designers using rapid prototyping over traditional
     jewellery making techniques?

          I.     Complicated designs can be more easily achieved
          II.    Customers are more satisfied with the product
          III.   Materials wastage is minimized
          IV.    Jewellery pieces are produced more efficiently

     A.   I and II only

     B.   I and III only

     C.   I, II, and III only

     D.   I, III, and IV only
```

- Roman numerals, indented, no terminal punctuation.
- Three or four statements. With three statements, the standard option set is `I and II only` / `I and III only` / `II and III only` / `I, II, III`.
- Statements are short and independently judgeable.

### 5.4 Figure-led items

Some items carry no prose at all, only a figure and a stem. Specimen SL 6(b) and 6(e) do this: a diagram or a survey result chart, then the question. These test data interpretation and are a good way to hit AO3 in a multiple-choice format. Include at least one per paper. Good candidates:

- A survey chart with a question about what further research is needed
- An exploded or sectioned diagram with a question about a named component
- An annotated student prototype photograph with a question about what the testing shows
- A process infographic with a missing stage

## 6. Syllabus coverage

Content is drawn from all three strands: **design in theory (A)**, **design in practice (B)**, **design in context (C)**.

| | A. Design in theory | B. Design in practice | C. Design in context |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **1. People** | A1.1 Ergonomics | B1.1 User-centred design | C1.1 Responsibility of the designer<br>C1.2 Inclusive design<br>**C1.3 Beyond usability (HL)** |
| **2. Process** | A2.1 User-centred research methods<br>A2.2 Prototyping techniques | B2.1 The design process<br>B2.2 Modelling and prototyping | C2.1 Design for sustainability<br>C2.2 Design for a circular economy |
| **3. Product** | A3.1 Material classification and properties<br>**A3.2 Structural systems (HL)**<br>**A3.3 Mechanical systems (HL)**<br>**A3.4 Electronic systems (HL)** | B3.1 Material selection<br>**B3.2 Structural systems application (HL)**<br>**B3.3 Mechanical systems application (HL)**<br>**B3.4 Electronic systems application (HL)** | C3.1 Product analysis and evaluation<br>**C3.2 Life-cycle analysis (HL)** |
| **4. Production** | **A4.1 Manufacturing techniques (HL)** | **B4.1 Production systems (HL)** | **C4.1 Design for manufacture strategies (HL)** |

**The SL/HL rule is absolute.** An SL Paper 1 may not contain a single item drawn from an HL-only sub-topic. Gears, structural calculation, electronic systems, production systems, manufacturing technique classification, DFM strategies, life-cycle analysis and beyond-usability are all HL only. An SL mock containing a gear-ratio question is invalid.

An HL Paper 1 draws on both. In the HL specimen, HL-only content carries roughly a third of the marks: gear systems (10b), compressive strength and structural behaviour (8a), static forces and Young's modulus (13c, 13g), ECU functions (13h), 3D printing technology classification (9d), subtractive/additive/joining/finishing (9c), robotics in production (13f), DFM strategies (8c, 12d, 13i), cradle-to-cradle and LCA (11b).

### Coverage targets

Build a coverage grid before writing and fill it in as you go.

- No sub-topic should carry more than about 15% of the paper.
- Every one of the four rows (People, Process, Product, Production) should appear. Production is HL only, so at SL the grid is three rows.
- Roughly half the items should be AO1/AO2 (recall and application) and half AO3 (analyse and evaluate). In multiple choice, AO3 items are the ones asking the candidate to rank, judge, compare or infer from data rather than retrieve.

## 7. House style

The IB has a specific written register. Match it.

| Convention | Rule | Specimen evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Spelling | British, with `-ize` endings | `optimize`, `minimized`, `personalize`, `behaviour`, `colour`, `jewellery`, `moulded`, `aluminium` |
| Hyphenated term | `user-centred design (UCD)` | HL P1 Q2 |
| Percentages | Space before the sign | `20 %`, `37.5 %`, `42 %`, `96 %` |
| Units | Metric first, imperial in brackets or square brackets | `2700 litres [approximately 594 gallons]`, `358 miles (576 km)` |
| Dimensions | Multiplication sign, spaced | `210 mm × 297 mm`, `8 × 8` |
| Numbers | Space as thousands separator | `$32 000`, `$38 000`, `66 prefabricated modules` |

| Figure caption | `Figure 4: Affordable housing development` | throughout |
| Continuation | `(This question continues on the following page)` at the foot, `(Question 9 continued)` at the head | throughout |

## 8. The markscheme

Paper 1 markschemes are a bare answer key. No rationale, no partial credit, nothing else. Reproduce this format:

```
Markscheme
Specimen paper
Design technology
Higher level
Paper 1

1.    B    9d.   C         12f.   B
2.    D    9e.   D         13a.   A
3.    D   10a.   B         13b.   B
...
```

Three columns, question reference then letter. Sub-parts written `8a.`, `9d.`, `13i.` with no brackets and no space before the letter.

For a school mock it is worth producing a **second, private document** giving the rationale and the intended sub-topic for each item, because it is what makes the mock useful for feedback. That document is not part of the paper.

## 9. Answer key balance

The observed keys:

| | A | B | C | D |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| HL specimen (40 items) | 9 | 14 | 8 | 9 |
| SL specimen (30 items) | 5 | 13 | 10 | **2** |

The HL distribution is acceptable. **The SL distribution is not a model to follow.** Two D answers in thirty items is a guessing exploit, and it is almost certainly an artefact of one specimen rather than a policy. When you write a mock:

- Keep each letter between 20% and 30% of items.
- Avoid runs of more than three identical letters.
- Do not let the key follow a visible pattern.
- Assign the key letter *after* writing the item, by shuffling the options, rather than habitually placing the correct answer second.

## 10. Build order

Work in this sequence. It prevents the most common failure, which is a paper that covers whatever the writer found easy to ask.

1. **Draw the coverage grid.** Sub-topics down the side, target item count across. Decide AO split.
2. **Choose case studies** (§4). Six or seven products for HL, five or six for SL. Check that between them they cover the grid. Check the SL/HL scope rule.
3. **Write the case study text and source the figures.** Fact-load each case (§4.3). Number figures sequentially across the whole paper, not per question.
4. **Write items against each case**, ticking off the grid. Anchor every stem (§3).
5. **Write the distractors last**, deliberately, using the taxonomy in §5.2.
6. **Shuffle option order** and record the key.
7. **Balance the key** (§9).
8. **Set the paper**: cover sheet, page furniture, continuation lines, reference list.
9. **Run the checklist** (§11).

## 11. Pre-release checklist

Structure

- [ ] Item count equals the paper total (30 SL, 40 HL) and every item is 1 mark
- [ ] Paper opens with a run of single-mark contextualized items
- [ ] Paper closes with a long case study of eight or nine parts
- [ ] Case study part counts resemble the specimen shape (§3)

Case studies

- [ ] Every question belongs to a case study or a named context. No orphan items.
- [ ] Every case-study stem names the product and points at a figure
- [ ] At least two thirds of items are unanswerable without the case
- [ ] No case body exceeds roughly 150 words
- [ ] Each multi-part case spans at least three syllabus sub-topics
- [ ] Every subject passes the §4.5 screening test, and any reuse asks about different sub-topics
- [ ] Each use recorded in the §4.6 subject bank
- [ ] At least one figure-led, data-interpretation item
- [ ] Figures numbered sequentially across the paper; all captioned; all referenced in the back matter

Scope

- [ ] SL paper contains **zero** HL-only content
- [ ] HL paper draws on HL content for roughly a third of items
- [ ] Coverage grid filled; no sub-topic above ~15% of the paper
- [ ] Roughly half the items sit at AO3

Items

- [ ] Four options, `A.`–`D.`, no terminal punctuation, capital first letter
- [ ] Options grammatically parallel and comparable in length
- [ ] Correct option is not the longest
- [ ] No "all of the above", "none of the above", "both A and B"
- [ ] No unflagged negation
- [ ] One or two multi-statement roman-numeral items, no more
- [ ] Each distractor traceable to a real misconception

Key and style

- [ ] Each key letter 20–30% of items; no run longer than three
- [ ] Markscheme is a bare answer key in three columns
- [ ] British spelling with `-ize`; `20 %` spacing; `×` for dimensions; `$32 000` number spacing
- [ ] Abbreviations expanded on first use
- [ ] Timing sanity check: HL 90 min ÷ 40 items ≈ 2¼ min; SL 60 min ÷ 30 items = 2 min

## 12. Worked examples

### 12.1 A three-part case study, HL

> **11.** A pioneering US furniture design company designed the Mirra Chair using the company's "design for the sustainable environment protocol".
>
> Design for manufacture (DFM) is at the core of this protocol, which aims to produce products that prioritize economic and environmental responsibilities through sustainable design principles.
>
> The Mirra chair (Figure 11), is made from 42 % of recycled materials and has won several Ecodesign awards. At the end of its useful life, 96 % of its weight is recyclable and it can be taken apart in 15 minutes.
>
> The design iterations of this product were evaluated using an environmental rating tool, based on: (1) the weight of the components, (2) the safety of the materials, (3) the recycled content, (4) the recyclability, and (5) the disassembly characteristics.
>
> **Figure 11: The Mirra Chair by Herman Miller**
>
> **(a)** Identify the aspect of the ACT model that relates to the ease of disassembly for the customer?
>
> A. Attract  B. Converse  C. Transact  D. All strands
>
> **(b)** What is the correct term for Miller's design philosophy in the designing and manufacturing of the Mirra chair (Figure 11)?
>
> A. Cradle-to-cradle  B. Cradle-to-grave  C. Life cycle analysis (LCA)  D. Design for process
>
> **(c)** When designing the Mirra Chair (Figure 11), which material property is most important to ensure proper spinal support and posture alignment?
>
> A. Compressive strength  B. Hardness  C. Elasticity  D. Thermal expansion

Why it works: the "taken apart in 15 minutes" fact in the body drives (a); the recyclability and rating-tool facts drive (b); (c) reaches into A3.1 with an ergonomic framing that only makes sense for a chair. Three sub-topics, one 110-word stimulus. Note (a) reproduces a real defect worth avoiding: it begins with the command word `Identify` but ends in a question mark. Write either `Identify the aspect ... customer.` or `Which aspect ... customer?`

### 12.2 Turning a weak item into an anchored one

**Weak:** Which manufacturing process is additive?
A. Injection moulding  B. Selective laser sintering  C. CNC milling  D. Die casting

**Anchored,** using the specimen's own case:

> **(d)** *Figure 9: Metal jewellery in production*
>
> What is the correct 3D printing technology for creating jewellery components using metal powder?
>
> A. Stereolithography  B. Fused deposition modelling (FDM)  C. Selective laser sintering (SLS)  D. Computer numerical control (CNC)

The distractors are now the adjacent processes a jewellery designer would actually be choosing between, and the stem's "metal powder" is the discriminator.
