Electronics Design

Statement of Inquiry: To be added from the G10 unit plan.

Students design and build an electronic product using microcontrollers, sensors and output devices. The unit introduces circuit design, programming logic and the input-process-output model, culminating in a working prototype that responds to real-world conditions, in preparation for DP Design.

Unit OverviewStudents follow the complete design cycle through an extended, independent electronics design project: identifying a genuine need and researching it in depth, planning with success criteria and specifications, designing, building and programming a solution, and critically evaluating the process and outcome in preparation for DP Design.

Suggested Lessons: Skills Training

… Skills training lessons to be added

Lesson details will be added when the G10 unit documents are available.

Each student is assigned a real client group inside the school and has to argue that the group needs something, and that the something needs to be electronic. They then plan their research against a fixed budget, work out what is inside four existing products without opening any of them, and pull the whole thing together into a brief.

Client assignment. Students draw a demographic brief card naming a real group in the school: the Grade 6 form class, the library lunch crowd, the ESL support group, the Grade 12 common room, the after school care group, the front office staff, the art room, the PE storeroom. Cards are curated by the teacher against what each student says they want to build, the same way the Grade 9 unit does it. The audience has to be someone other than themselves.

MYP descriptor, 7 to 8Explains and justifies the need for a solution to a problem for a client/target audience.
1 classIndividualHand in: Need Statement, 1 page

Students identify, explain and justify the need for an electronic device for the group on their card. The justification is the whole strand, so the writing has to argue rather than describe.

What goes on the page

  • Who the audience is, in specifics. Not "the library crowd" but what fourteen of them are doing at 12:40 on a Wednesday.
  • The situation as it stands, with at least one piece of evidence gathered rather than assumed.
  • Why this has to be electronic. What the device will sense, work out, or remember that a person, a label, a printed chart or a phone the client already owns cannot do as well here.
  • What happens if nothing is made. If the honest answer is "nothing much", the need has not been found yet.

Core test

The sticky note test

Before a need is accepted, it has to survive one question: would a sticky note do this? A pencil, a label, a printed sign, a timer already on the wall, or an app the client has on their phone. If any of those solves the problem better, the electronics are not justified and the project is a gadget looking for an excuse.

A need passes when the problem has a sensing, timing, counting or remembering shape to it. Something has to be measured that a person cannot watch all day, or counted that a person would lose track of, or noticed at a moment when nobody is there to notice it. That shape is what makes electronics the right answer rather than the fun one.

The 5-6 to 7-8 lineA 5-6 explains that the audience exists and that a device would be useful to them. A 7-8 argues why this audience, why this need, and why the problem has a shape that electronics fits and a sticky note does not.
Formative · 15 min The sticky note test Ten problems on cards. For each, decide whether electronics is justified or whether a label, a timer, a person or a phone would do it better. The founding error of the whole unit, caught in one activity.
Formative · 15 min Need or want sort Twelve statement cards about a demographic, sorted into need, want and assumption. The assumption pile is the point. Carried over from the Grade 9 unit, so most students will know the routine.
Formative · 15 min Weak brief autopsy A deliberately vague need statement. In pairs, find the three claims that are asserted but never justified. Faster than explaining what justification looks like, because they see it by its absence.

Common failure

Arriving with the device already decided

Students turn up wanting to build a thing, then reverse engineer a need to fit it. The fix is a ban: no component may be named in the first draft. If the need statement cannot be written without the word "sensor", it is a product pitch and not a need.

MYP descriptor, 7 to 8Constructs a detailed research plan, which identifies and prioritizes the primary and secondary research needed to develop a solution to the problem independently.
1 classIndividualHand in: Research Plan, 1 page

The awkward strand, because MYP Design has nowhere to grade a research paper. The plan itself has to be the assessed artefact, and it has to visibly prioritise rather than list. The token budget from Grade 9 carries over with one addition that turns it into electronics research.

Task device

The research budget

Each student gets 12 research tokens. Every activity has a price, and nobody can afford everything.

ActivityCostPrimary or secondaryGives you
Read a catalog part page and note what it can and cannot do1SecondaryWhat the hardware will and will not do
Any other secondary source: datasheet, teardown video, product page, guide1SecondaryBackground, fast, shallow
Questionnaire2PrimaryNumbers from many people
Structured observation of the audience3PrimaryWhat people do, not what they say
Interview3PrimaryDepth from one person
Bench test: breadboard a part and measure whether it does what you need3PrimaryWhether the design is possible at all
Focus group4PrimaryDisagreement between people

Two spending rules, which stop the two predictable failures. At least 4 tokens on people, or the whole plan is datasheets. At least 3 tokens on technical feasibility, meaning a bench test or two part studies, or the whole plan is opinions and build class 4 discovers the sensor cannot do the thing.

Why the bench test is hereIt is primary research, it generates real data, it is unavailable to a student who only reads, and it is the row that most often changes a design while changing it is still free. A student who spends 3 tokens finding out that the ultrasonic sensor cannot see a person sitting still has just bought themselves a working project.

One page, one row per activity

Question I need answeredMethodPrimary / secondaryCostPriorityWhat decision this unlocksWhen
Eight to twelve rows is typical. The budget is what stops it being twenty.

Two short paragraphs sit under the table:

  • Why these, in this order. Which activity had to happen first, and what it unblocks.
  • What I gave up. The activity that could not be afforded, and the risk that creates.
The column that does the work"What decision this unlocks" is what turns priority from an opinion into an argument. A row that unlocks no decision should not be on the plan at all.
Formative · 20 min Spend the tokens A practice run of the budget against a fake brief for a different audience, before doing it for real. Compare spends across the room and argue about them.
Formative · 10 min Question quality ladder Rank six research questions from useless to sharp. "Do people like gadgets?" against "How long does a Grade 6 stay at a desk before they get up?" The difference between them is the lesson.
Formative · 10 min What a bench test buys you Two research plans, one with a bench test in it and one without. Both reach build class 4. Work out what happens to each. The fastest way to make 3 tokens on hardware feel worth spending.

Planned

Interactive: research budget planner

Drag activities into a plan, watch the token total, and get blocked when overspending. Shared with the Grade 9 unit, with the electronics activity list swapped in. Exports the finished table.

MYP descriptor, 7 to 8Analyses a range of existing products that inspire a solution to the problem in detail.
3 classesIndividualHand in: 4 Black Box Cards + synthesis

Four teardowns, then one synthesis page. No product is opened. The work is inferring the system from the outside, which is harder and far more useful than unscrewing something, and it uses the parts catalog as the shared vocabulary the way the mechanics catalog works in Grade 9.

Required range, one of each

  1. A product in the room, in their bag, or at home, that can be picked up and handled
  2. A product aimed at, or usable by, the group on their brief card
  3. A product with no screen, which therefore has to talk to its user through light, sound, movement or vibration
  4. A product that is badly designed, chosen deliberately

What goes on a Black Box Card

  • What it is, who it is for, what it costs, how it is powered.
  • What it must be sensing, and the evidence for that guess. A device that wakes when you approach is sensing something. Which catalog sensors could produce that behaviour, and which are ruled out by what you observed?
  • What it must be deciding. The behaviour that cannot be explained by a wire running straight from input to output.
  • What it does back, and through which output.
  • Inferred block diagram, drawn with catalog parts named, so the claim is specific enough to be wrong.
  • Power: mains, battery or rechargeable, and what that choice forced on the rest of the design.
  • Interface: every control and every indicator, and what the user has to already know in order to use it.
  • One sentence on what to take from this product and what to leave.

Checking the guess. Where a teardown video or an iFixit entry exists, students find it and record where their inference was right and where it was wrong. Being wrong and saying so is worth more than a vague card that cannot be checked. Say this out loud before they start, or every card will hedge.

The synthesis page

A comparison matrix across all four products against the same criteria, ending in three to five numbered design implications, each written as "Because..., my device should...". These get quoted directly in Aiv and Bi, so they need to be sharp.

The 5-6 to 7-8 lineFour good cards with no synthesis is a 5-6, because the analysis has not been carried anywhere. The implications are the difference.
Electronics Parts Catalog 49 parts, 9 categories
Formative · 30 min Guess the block diagram, together Lead a full black box teardown of one device from the front, thinking aloud, including the parts you are unsure of. Then play the teardown video and mark your own guesses in public. Models the depth expected, and models being wrong.
Formative · 15 min Name that part Show a behaviour. Students name every catalog part that could produce it, then rule out the ones that could not and say why. Builds the shared vocabulary at almost no time cost.
Formative · 20 min The badly designed thing Bring in something genuinely annoying. Locate the failure precisely: is it the sensor, the decision, the feedback, or the interface? Turns complaining into analysis, which is the whole strand.

Class shape

1Teardown together, then the handheld product

Model a full black box teardown on the board, check it against a video, then students complete card 1 on something they can physically hold.

2Two more cards

The audience product and the screenless product. Students who cannot get hold of a physical unit work from the catalog, the product page, the manual and video.

3The badly designed one, then the synthesis

Card 4, then the comparison matrix and the design implications. This is the class that decides the quality of the strand, so protect the time for it.

MYP descriptor, 7 to 8Develops a detailed design brief, which summarizes the analysis of relevant research.
1 classIndividualHand in: Design Brief, 1 page

Note the verb. The brief summarises the analysis, so this is a synthesis document. Measurable criteria belong in Bi, not here. Keeping the two apart is the single most common A to B confusion, and the formative below exists for that alone.

One page, with an evidence column

SectionContentEvidence
AudienceWho they are, and what matters about them hereAi, Aii
The needRestated in one sentenceAi
What the research showedThree to five findings, human and technicalAii, Aiii
Design implicationsCarried across from the Aiii synthesisAiii
Technical envelopeWhat it must sense, what it must decide, what it must do. Where the power comes from. Where it livesAii, Aiii
ConstraintsTime, parts available, cost ceiling, the board issued, the skills heldGiven
Design intentOne paragraph on what will be made and for whomSynthesis

The evidence column is compulsory. Every claim points at a source, so a brief that has drifted away from the research becomes visible immediately.

The electronics additionThe technical envelope row is the bridge from research to specification. It is where the research stops being about people and starts being about what the device has to do, and it is the row that Bi turns into numbers.

Optional flourish

Write the design intent as the back of the box

Same content, retail packaging format: product name, one line of promise, three bullet features, a spec strip along the bottom. On theme, it forces brevity, and it prints well for the portfolio. Keep the evidence table as the graded part.

Formative · 10 min Brief or spec? Ten statements, sorted into "belongs in Aiv" and "belongs in Bi". Fixes the confusion before it costs marks in both strands.
