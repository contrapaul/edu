Electronics Design

Unit OverviewEach student is assigned a real client group in the school and has to argue that the group needs something that has to be electronic. They research it, analyse four existing products without opening any of them, specify the device in measurable terms, design and draw it, build it, and then test it with the people it was made for. The unit is assessed as sixteen separate strands rather than four summative tasks, so every part of the design cycle carries its own grade and its own deadline.

Every strand goes to ManageBac as it finishes, including the strands that are also assessed live in class. The bench block, classes 1 to 3

Not assessed, but everything in Criterion B assumes it. Three drills, each ending in something that works on a breadboard. Students who can already do this run the extension instead of sitting through it.

1Blink and measure

LED, resistor, breadboard, board. Then measure the rail with a multimeter and read the current.

What it establishes. Power and ground become a habit rather than a step, and the multimeter becomes a normal object rather than a special occasion.

2Sense, decide, act

An LDR or a button in, an LED or a buzzer out, and a threshold in between that the student picks and then changes.

What it establishes. The unit's first non-negotiable, felt in the hand before it is ever written into a specification.

3Break it and fix it

Teacher-broken circuits and teacher-broken code at four stations. Students diagnose using the troubleshooting protocol below.

What it establishes. Troubleshooting as a method, rather than as re-prompting until something works.

Class routine

The troubleshooting protocol

Taught in bench class 3 and posted on the wall for the rest of the unit. Students write it into the bug diary in this order.

  1. What did I expect, and what actually happened? Be specific about both.
  2. Is it power, wiring, or code? Prove which before changing anything.
  3. What is the smallest test that would tell me? Run that one.
  4. Change one thing. Test. Write down the result.
  5. Only now ask for help, and bring answers to 1 through 4 with you.

Step 5 is the AI rule as well. Asking a model to "fix this" without steps 1 to 4 is exactly what the bug diary is designed to expose.

Two requirements sit above everything else. Both are written into the specification in Bi, then tested in Di and Dii. A device that misses either one cannot reach the top band, however well it is made. Teach them in the launch class and return to them constantly.

Rule 1

It senses, decides, and acts

Three parts, all required. A device that maps an input straight to an output has no decision in it, and a button wired to an LED is not a design.

There must be at least one thing the device works out for itself: a threshold, a count, a timer, a remembered state, a comparison between now and a moment ago. The student must be able to point at the decision and say what would happen if it were removed.

Rule 2

It survives being unplugged from you

At handover the device works on the client's desk, on its own power, with no laptop attached, no serial monitor open, and no wire held in place by a finger.

Nobody stands next to it explaining. Enclosed, labelled, switched on, working. This is the difference between a project and a demonstration.

Watch for

The device that would have been better as a sticky note

The most common failure in an electronics unit is a device nobody needed. If a pencil, a label, a timer already on the wall or an app the client owns would solve the problem better, the electronics are not justified. Catch it in Ai, where the sticky note test lives, and not in Dii when the thing is already built.

A language model will write working firmware for any student who asks it to. This is fine and intended: students start from zero, the interesting design work is elsewhere, and refusing the tool would only move its use out of sight. But Cii says "demonstrates excellent technical skills", and pasted code demonstrates nothing. Four devices carry the technical grade instead.

1. Behaviour is specified before code exists

Biv requires a state diagram and a written firmware plan. The student designs what the device does; the model writes the syntax that does it. A student who cannot draw the state diagram has not designed anything, and this shows up in B, before the build.

2. The code viva

Twice in the build block the teacher points at three lines of the student's own firmware and asks what each does and what breaks if it changes. Two minutes per student. Unglamorous and completely decisive.

3. The bug diary

Every debugging episode logged: symptom, what I thought was wrong, what I tried, what actually happened. A model can produce code. It cannot produce the record of a student narrowing down their own fault.

4. Hardware does not lie

Wiring, soldering, current draw, enclosure fit and connector strain relief are assisted by nothing. Three of the four Cii skill tracks are physical for exactly this reason.

Say all four out loud in the launch class. The message is that using the model well is expected, and that the assessment sits in the places the model cannot reach. Told in advance it is a rule. Discovered at the first viva it is a trap, and students will treat every later instruction as one.

Each student is assigned a real client group inside the school and has to argue that the group needs something, and that the something needs to be electronic. They then plan their research against a fixed budget, work out what is inside four existing products without opening any of them, and pull the whole thing together into a brief.

Client assignment. Students draw a demographic brief card naming a real group in the school: the Grade 6 form class, the library lunch crowd, the ESL support group, the Grade 12 common room, the after school care group, the front office staff, the art room, the PE storeroom. Cards are curated by the teacher against what each student says they want to build, the same way the Grade 9 unit does it. The audience has to be someone other than themselves.

MYP descriptor, 7 to 8Explains and justifies the need for a solution to a problem for a client/target audience.
1 classIndividualHand in: Need Statement, 1 page

Students identify, explain and justify the need for an electronic device for the group on their card. The justification is the whole strand, so the writing has to argue rather than describe.

What goes on the page

  • Who the audience is, in specifics. Not "the library crowd" but what fourteen of them are doing at 12:40 on a Wednesday.
  • The situation as it stands, with at least one piece of evidence gathered rather than assumed.
  • Why this has to be electronic. What the device will sense, work out, or remember that a person, a label, a printed chart or a phone the client already owns cannot do as well here.
  • What happens if nothing is made. If the honest answer is "nothing much", the need has not been found yet.

Core test

The sticky note test

Before a need is accepted, it has to survive one question: would a sticky note do this? A pencil, a label, a printed sign, a timer already on the wall, or an app the client has on their phone. If any of those solves the problem better, the electronics are not justified and the project is a gadget looking for an excuse.

A need passes when the problem has a sensing, timing, counting or remembering shape to it. Something has to be measured that a person cannot watch all day, or counted that a person would lose track of, or noticed at a moment when nobody is there to notice it. That shape is what makes electronics the right answer rather than the fun one.

The 5-6 to 7-8 lineA 5-6 explains that the audience exists and that a device would be useful to them. A 7-8 argues why this audience, why this need, and why the problem has a shape that electronics fits and a sticky note does not.
Formative · 15 min The sticky note test Ten problems on cards. For each, decide whether electronics is justified or whether a label, a timer, a person or a phone would do it better. The founding error of the whole unit, caught in one activity.
Formative · 15 min Need or want sort Twelve statement cards about a demographic, sorted into need, want and assumption. The assumption pile is the point. Carried over from the Grade 9 unit, so most students will know the routine.
Formative · 15 min Weak brief autopsy A deliberately vague need statement. In pairs, find the three claims that are asserted but never justified. Faster than explaining what justification looks like, because they see it by its absence.

Common failure

Arriving with the device already decided

Students turn up wanting to build a thing, then reverse engineer a need to fit it. The fix is a ban: no component may be named in the first draft. If the need statement cannot be written without the word "sensor", it is a product pitch and not a need.

MYP descriptor, 7 to 8Constructs a detailed research plan, which identifies and prioritizes the primary and secondary research needed to develop a solution to the problem independently.
1 classIndividualHand in: Research Plan, 1 page

The awkward strand, because MYP Design has nowhere to grade a research paper. The plan itself has to be the assessed artefact, and it has to visibly prioritise rather than list. The token budget from Grade 9 carries over with one addition that turns it into electronics research.

Task device

The research budget

Each student gets 12 research tokens. Every activity has a price, and nobody can afford everything.

Two spending rules, which stop the two predictable failures. At least 4 tokens on people, or the whole plan is datasheets. At least 3 tokens on technical feasibility, meaning a bench test or two part studies, or the whole plan is opinions and build class 4 discovers the sensor cannot do the thing.

Why the bench test is hereIt is primary research, it generates real data, it is unavailable to a student who only reads, and it is the row that most often changes a design while changing it is still free. A student who spends 3 tokens finding out that the ultrasonic sensor cannot see a person sitting still has just bought themselves a working project.

One page, one row per activity

Question I need answeredMethodPrimary / secondaryCostPriorityWhat decision this unlocksWhen
Eight to twelve rows is typical. The budget is what stops it being twenty.

Two short paragraphs sit under the table:

  • Why these, in this order. Which activity had to happen first, and what it unblocks.
  • What I gave up. The activity that could not be afforded, and the risk that creates.
The column that does the work"What decision this unlocks" is what turns priority from an opinion into an argument. A row that unlocks no decision should not be on the plan at all.
Formative · 20 min Spend the tokens A practice run of the budget against a fake brief for a different audience, before doing it for real. Compare spends across the room and argue about them.
Formative · 10 min Question quality ladder Rank six research questions from useless to sharp. "Do people like gadgets?" against "How long does a Grade 6 stay at a desk before they get up?" The difference between them is the lesson.
Formative · 10 min What a bench test buys you Two research plans, one with a bench test in it and one without. Both reach build class 4. Work out what happens to each. The fastest way to make 3 tokens on hardware feel worth spending.

MYP descriptor, 7 to 8Analyses a range of existing products that inspire a solution to the problem in detail.
3 classesIndividualHand in: 4 Black Box Cards + synthesis

Four teardowns, then one synthesis page. No product is opened. The work is inferring the system from the outside, which is harder and far more useful than unscrewing something, and it uses the parts catalog as the shared vocabulary the way the mechanics catalog works in Grade 9.

Required range, one of each

  1. A product in the room, in their bag, or at home, that can be picked up and handled
  2. A product aimed at, or usable by, the group on their brief card
  3. A product with no screen, which therefore has to talk to its user through light, sound, movement or vibration
  4. A product that is badly designed, chosen deliberately

What goes on a Black Box Card

  • What it is, who it is for, what it costs, how it is powered.
  • What it must be sensing, and the evidence for that guess. A device that wakes when you approach is sensing something. Which catalog sensors could produce that behaviour, and which are ruled out by what you observed?
  • What it must be deciding. The behaviour that cannot be explained by a wire running straight from input to output.
  • What it does back, and through which output.
  • Inferred block diagram, drawn with catalog parts named, so the claim is specific enough to be wrong.
  • Power: mains, battery or rechargeable, and what that choice forced on the rest of the design.
  • Interface: every control and every indicator, and what the user has to already know in order to use it.
  • One sentence on what to take from this product and what to leave.

Checking the guess. Where a teardown video or an iFixit entry exists, students find it and record where their inference was right and where it was wrong. Being wrong and saying so is worth more than a vague card that cannot be checked. Say this out loud before they start, or every card will hedge.

The synthesis page

A comparison matrix across all four products against the same criteria, ending in three to five numbered design implications, each written as "Because..., my device should...". These get quoted directly in Aiv and Bi, so they need to be sharp.

The 5-6 to 7-8 lineFour good cards with no synthesis is a 5-6, because the analysis has not been carried anywhere. The implications are the difference.
Electronics Parts Catalog 49 parts, 9 categories
Formative · 30 min Guess the block diagram, together Lead a full black box teardown of one device from the front, thinking aloud, including the parts you are unsure of. Then play the teardown video and mark your own guesses in public. Models the depth expected, and models being wrong.
Formative · 15 min Name that part Show a behaviour. Students name every catalog part that could produce it, then rule out the ones that could not and say why. Builds the shared vocabulary at almost no time cost.
Formative · 20 min The badly designed thing Bring in something genuinely annoying. Locate the failure precisely: is it the sensor, the decision, the feedback, or the interface? Turns complaining into analysis, which is the whole strand.

The audience product and the screenless product. Students who cannot get hold of a physical unit work from the catalog, the product page, the manual and video.

The electronics additionThe technical envelope row is the bridge from research to specification. It is where the research stops being about people and starts being about what the device has to do, and it is the row that Bi turns into numbers.

Optional flourish

Write the design intent as the back of the box

Same content, retail packaging format: product name, one line of promise, three bullet features, a spec strip along the bottom. On theme, it forces brevity, and it prints well for the portfolio. Keep the evidence table as the graded part.

Formative · 10 min Brief or spec? Ten statements, sorted into "belongs in Aiv" and "belongs in Bi". Fixes the confusion before it costs marks in both strands.

The research becomes a specification with numbers in it, then a range of real concepts, then one chosen design that has survived three technical gates, then a production pack complete enough to build from. This is the longest criterion in the unit and the one that decides whether Criterion C is a build or a scramble.

Three hard rules

What makes a row count as a specification

  1. If it cannot be measured, it is not a specification. "Responsive" fails. "Reacts within 0.5 seconds, measured over 20 trials" passes.
  2. The test column is written now, not in Di. Di expands it into real instruments. Writing it here is what stops untestable specifications from existing in the first place.
  3. Every part named in a specification links to its catalog page, using the deep link, for example tools/electronics/#hc-sr04. A specification that depends on a part should point at what that part can actually do. This is what keeps a spec honest about the hardware.
Not optionalThe two non-negotiables appear here as rows: one specification for the decision the device makes, and one for working unattended on its own power at the client's desk. A specification page without both is sent back before the strand is graded.
Formative · 20 min Make it measurable Eight unmeasurable specs. Rewrite each with a number, a unit and a test. Kills "responsive", "reliable" and "user friendly" in one sitting.
Formative · 15 min Spec to test matching Given a spec, choose the method that could actually test it and name the instrument it needs. Sets up Di eight classes early, which is when it is cheap.
Formative · 15 min Find the untestable spec A specification page with three rows that cannot be tested by anyone. Find them, then fix them. Rule one, applied to someone else's work first.

MYP descriptor, 7 to 8Develops a range of feasible design ideas, using an appropriate medium(s) and detailed annotation, which can be correctly interpreted by others.
3 classesIndividualHand in: 6 concepts + 2 breadboard proofs

Three parts. A forced draw that produces genuine range, six annotated concepts on a standard sheet, and two ideas actually breadboarded, because in electronics an untested idea is not a feasible one.

Task device

The double draw

Each student draws two cards, one from the input deck and one from the output deck, and must generate a concept using both. Draw three times.

Input deckOutput deck
Something the user does on purpose: button, dial, slider, joystick, switchA screen
Something about the room: light, temperature, humidity, soundLight, single or addressable
Something about a person: presence, motion, proximity, touchSound
Something about an object: tilt, magnet, weight, vibrationMovement, servo or vibration
Time itself: elapsed, scheduled, countedSomething sent elsewhere, over USB or wireless

Across the full set of concepts a student must touch at least three different input rows and three different output rows. This produces range rather than six versions of the first idea, which is what the descriptor is actually asking for.

Six annotated concepts, four things on every sheet

A standard A3 template, so annotation has somewhere to live and the sheet is learned once. Every concept carries these four in the same places.

  • Block diagram. Input, process, output, with the actual catalog parts named. The process box says what is decided, not "ESP32".
  • Form sketch. The physical object at rough scale, in its place. Where it sits, what it mounts to, how big it is next to a hand.
  • Interaction storyboard, three frames. What the user does, what the device does back, and what state it is left in. This is the frame that catches devices which work but cannot be understood.
  • Annotation on how the concept serves the audience, referencing Bi specs by number, plus one named risk: the part most likely to fail, and why.

Hand drawing is the better medium here. MYP rewards annotation density and students annotate more freely by hand. Digital is allowed where the annotation is as rich.

Two breadboard proofsFor two different concepts, breadboard the single riskiest part for twenty minutes: the sensor that might not read what you need, the display that might be too slow, the servo that might not have the torque. Hand in a photo, the reading or behaviour observed, and one sentence on what it means for the concept. A proof that fails is worth full marks. It is feasibility evidence either way, and it is why this happens now rather than in build class 4.

Formative and evidence at once

The interpretation test

Swap sheets with a classmate. From the block diagram alone, with no talking from you, they say out loud which board pin each part would go to and what the code would have to check first. You write down everything they could not answer.

That list is submitted with the concepts. It is direct evidence for "can be correctly interpreted by others", which is otherwise the hardest phrase in the strand to evidence, and it is worth more than any assurance that the sheets are clear.

Formative · 30 min Double draw sprint Draw an input and an output card, sketch a concept in eight minutes. Three rounds. Fluency and range before either one counts for marks.
Formative · 20 min Block diagram drill Given a described device, draw the block diagram naming catalog parts. Six devices, increasing in difficulty. The core drawing of the whole unit, so drill it until it is quick.
Formative · 15 min Annotation density check Two sketches of the same idea, one bare and one annotated. Count what can only be learned from the annotated one. Annotation is the assessed part, not the drawing.
Formative · 20 min Interpretation rehearsal Wire a partner's block diagram on a breadboard from the sheet alone, with no talking. An ungraded first run, which reveals what a reader actually needs before it counts.

Class shape

1Double draw and rapid concepts

Three draws, three fast concepts each, no polish. Quantity first. Check the input and output coverage before anyone starts refining.

2Develop and annotate the best six

Onto the A3 template, all four required elements on each sheet. This is the class where the annotation gets written, so protect it from turning into a drawing lesson.

3Breadboard proofs and the interpretation test

Twenty minutes each on the two riskiest parts, then swap sheets. Both halves generate submitted evidence, so neither is optional.

MYP descriptor, 7 to 8Presents the chosen design and justifies fully and critically its selection with detailed reference to the design specification.
2 classesIndividualHand in: Gates + matrix + justification

The strand where students write "I picked this one because it is the best". Two fixes: put a technical gate in front of the choice so it cannot be a beauty contest, and make the case against the winner compulsory.

Part 1

The three feasibility gates

Before a concept can be chosen it is run through all three, and the working is shown. A concept may still be chosen after failing a gate, but then the justification has to say exactly how the gate will be cleared, by when, and at what cost.

GateThe checkFails when
PinsEvery part mapped to a specific pin on the issued ESP32-S3 SuperMini, using the catalog pin tables and the MacroPad BuilderMore parts than pins, two parts on one pin, or something sitting on a boot pin
PowerCurrent draw of every part added up at worst case, against what the supply or battery can give, with a runtime estimateServos and LED strips. Almost every time
PartsEvery part is in the room, in the catalog, or has a named source with a price and a lead timeSomething has to arrive from abroad in week three

The power gate teaches the most. A student who discovers that their five servos want more current than a USB port will give has learned something no amount of sketching would have taught them.

Part 2: the weighted decision matrix

Three finalist concepts scored against every Bi specification, with weights assigned and each weighting justified in a sentence. The weighting is where the judgement is, so the weighting is what gets graded, not the arithmetic.

Part 3: the critical justification, one to two pages

  • Why the chosen design wins, referencing specifications by number.
  • The case against it. What it does worse than the concepts rejected, and what is being given up. This section is what turns "justifies" into "justifies critically", and without it the ceiling is 5-6.
  • What was carried across from the rejected concepts into the final design.
  • What is still unresolved, including any gate not yet cleared, and how the build will resolve it.
The board caseA student who wants a board other than the issued SuperMini argues for it here, against the gates: what the SuperMini cannot do, what the alternative gives, what it costs, and when it must be ordered by. A real justification with a real answer at the end of it, which makes it the best possible practice for the strand itself.
Formative · 20 min Blow the power budget A parts list that draws more than the supply can give. Find the problem, then fix it three different ways. The gate that catches the most projects, met before it catches theirs.
Formative · 15 min Pin collision hunt A pin map with a boot pin used, two parts on one pin, and an analog part on a digital-only pin. Teaches the pins gate and how to read a catalog pin table at the same time.
Formative · 20 min Matrix with a rigged winner A decision matrix where the highest scoring option is obviously wrong. What did the matrix miss? Stops blind trust in a number that the student chose the weights for.
Formative · 15 min Write the case against Practise arguing against a design you like. The hardest half of Biii, and the half that decides the band.

Class shape

1Run the gates on all three finalists

Bench and calculator work, and it produces a result the student did not know in advance. Expect at least one concept to die here, which is the point of doing it before the matrix rather than after.

2The matrix and the written justification

By now the gates have usually decided more than the matrix has. Say that out loud: a matrix that merely confirms what the power budget already ruled out is doing no work, and a student who notices that is showing exactly the judgement the strand is asking for.

3 classesIndividualHand in: Production Pack, 6 to 9 pages

The page that carries the pack

Why the state diagram matters more than the schematic

It is the answer to the AI problem. A student who can draw every state their device can be in, and every event that moves between them, has done the design work, and a model writing the syntax afterwards changes nothing about that.

A student who cannot draw it does not yet have a design, and Biv is the right place to find that out, while it still costs a redraw rather than a rebuild. Teach it with three worked examples: a two state device, a device with a settings mode, and one with a timeout that returns it to rest.

Solder School runs alongsideBiv is desk work and tolerates rotation, so a bench of six runs a soldering clinic through each of these three classes while the rest draw. Every student is certified before build class 1, and the time costs nothing extra because it is already timetabled.

Route for a student who cannot solder safely. Screw terminals, crimped connectors, JST leads and header sockets carry the same marks in the Cii wiring track. A device may be built with no solder in it at all. Nothing in the assessment requires soldering, but everyone is taught it.

Formative · 25 min State diagram drill Draw the state diagram for three familiar objects: a microwave, a set of traffic lights, and a game controller that goes to sleep. Familiar behaviour, unfamiliar notation, which is the right way round.
Formative · 30 min Schematic to breadboard, and back Given a schematic, build it. Given a built breadboard, draw the schematic. Run both directions and the symbols stop being decoration.
Formative · 20 min Cutout disaster An enclosure drawing with the USB cutout 3 mm out and the screen window rotated. Find the errors before printing. Cheaper than finding them after.
Formative · 25 min Price a build Take a finished project and write its full bill of materials with real prices and real lead times. Cost is a specification, so it has to be a number somebody checked.
