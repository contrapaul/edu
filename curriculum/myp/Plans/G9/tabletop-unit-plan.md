# Grade 9 Tabletop Game Design: 16 strand assessment plan

Working document. Strand descriptors below are the 7-8 band, task specific.

## Unit parameters

| | |
|---|---|
| Grouping | Groups of 3 or 4 build one game. Every strand produces an **individual** artefact. |
| Length | 33 assessed classes plus a launch class and 2 slack, so 36 of a 30 to 40 budget. |
| Client | Real, arranged. A named group at school per team, **assigned by the teacher** to fit what the team says it wants to make. |
| Submission | Each strand submitted separately as it finishes, and added to a running portfolio. |
| Session length | **20 to 30 minutes**, targeting post-lunch recess. A game may span many sessions. |
| Rulebook | **4 pages maximum**, learnable inside one recess. Reference: [OnePageRules](https://onepagerules.com/). |
| Core requirement | The game **must offer meaningful choices**. See below. |
| Key tool | [Game Mechanics Catalog](https://edu.contrapaul.com/tools/mechanics/), 70 mechanics in 10 families. |

## The two non-negotiables

Both are written into the Bi specification and tested in Di and Dii, so a game
that misses either cannot reach the top band however well it is made.

**1. Meaningful choices.** A choice is meaningful only when all four hold: the
options are actually different; no option is always best; the player can reason
about it; and the consequence is visible. Four tests students can run on their
own game: the coin flip test, the dominant strategy check, the explain back test,
and the regret test. The failure mode to catch early is roll and move, and it has
to be caught in Bii, not in Dii when the game is already built.

**2. Fits a recess.** 20 to 30 minutes per session, and the rules must be
learnable in one recess, which is the real reason for the 4 page cap.

**The grouping problem, and how this plan solves it.** A group build makes C and D
authentic but makes individual evidence hard. Three devices carry the individual
grade through the group phase:

1. **Component ownership.** Every student is named owner of a specific subset of
   components in Ci, and only that student makes them.
2. **The making log.** Dated photographs with the student's own hand or initials
   visible on the part, plus a note on what went wrong.
3. **Individual writing on group decisions.** Biii, Civ, Dii, Diii and Div are
   written alone even when the decision was collective. A student who lost the
   group vote still writes their own justification of the direction taken.

# Criterion A: Inquiring and analysing

## Ai (1 class)

> Explains and justifies the need for a solution to a problem for a client/target audience.

**Task.** Each team draws a demographic brief card naming a real group at school:
Grade 6 form class, the library lunch crowd, the ESL support group, the Grade 12
common room, the after school care group, staff at break. Students identify,
explain and justify the need for a tabletop game for that audience.

**Deliverable.** One page Need Statement containing:
- Who the audience is, in specifics rather than generalities
- The situation as it currently stands, with at least one piece of evidence
- Why a tabletop game is the right response rather than something else
- What happens if nothing is made

**What separates 7-8 from 5-6.** The justification is the whole strand. A 5-6
explains that the audience exists and might enjoy a game. A 7-8 argues why this
audience, this need, and why a tabletop game specifically beats the alternatives.

**Common failure.** Writing about games they want to make rather than about the
audience. Ban the word "I" from the first draft.

## Aii (1 class)

> Constructs a detailed research plan, which identifies and prioritizes the primary and secondary research needed to develop a solution to the problem independently.

Your concern is right: MYP Design has no space to grade a research paper, so the
plan itself has to be the artefact and it has to visibly prioritise.

**Proposed task: the research budget.** Students receive 12 research tokens. Every
research activity has a token cost: a secondary source costs 1, a questionnaire
costs 2, a structured observation costs 3, an interview costs 3, a focus group
costs 4. They cannot afford everything. Spending forces genuine prioritisation
instead of a ranked list written after the fact.

**Deliverable.** One page Research Plan table, one row per activity:

| Question I need answered | Method | Primary or secondary | Cost | Priority | What decision this unlocks | When |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Plus two short paragraphs below the table:
- **Why these, in this order.** Which activity had to happen first and why.
- **What I gave up.** The activity they could not afford, and the risk that creates.

The "what decision this unlocks" column is the one that does the work. It turns
prioritisation from an opinion into an argument, and it is what a 7-8 needs.

**Alternative if you dislike the token device.** A must / should / could sort with
a mandatory dependency arrow between rows, so sequence is visible.

## Aiii (3 classes)

> Analyses a range of existing products that inspire a solution to the problem in detail.

**Task.** Four teardowns, then one synthesis. The mechanics catalog is the shared
vocabulary so that analysis stays specific.

Required range, one from each:
1. A game played in class
2. A game that uses a mechanic family the student has not used before
3. A game aimed at, or playable by, their demographic
4. Free choice, may be a video game if the mechanic transfers

**Deliverable, per game.** One Teardown Card:
- What it is, audience, player count, playtime, components
- Mechanics used, **named from the catalog**, with the family
- What works, for whom, and why it works
- What fails, and for whom
- One sentence: what I would take from this and what I would leave

**Deliverable, synthesis page.** A comparison matrix across all four against the
same criteria, ending in 3 to 5 numbered **design implications**. Each implication
is a sentence beginning "Because..., my game should...".

**What separates 7-8.** The synthesis. Four good teardowns with no synthesis is a
5-6, because analysis has not been carried forward. The implications are also what
Aiv and Bi will quote directly.

**Class shape.** Class 1 play and teardown two games. Class 2 teardown two more,
one researched online. Class 3 synthesis and implications.

## Aiv (1 class)

> Develops a detailed design brief, which summarizes the analysis of relevant research.

Note the verb: it **summarises the analysis**, so this is a synthesis document, not
a specification. Bi is where measurable criteria appear. Keeping these apart is the
main thing students get wrong.

**Deliverable.** One page Design Brief with a mandatory evidence column, so every
claim points back at a source:

| Section | Content | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Audience | Who, and what matters about them | Ai, Aii |
| The need | Restated in one sentence | Ai |
| What the research showed | 3 to 5 findings | Aii, Aiii |
| Design implications | Carried from the Aiii synthesis | Aiii |
| Constraints | Time, budget, materials, table size, session length | Given |
| Design intent | One paragraph on what will be made and for whom | Synthesis |

**Optional flourish.** Present the design intent paragraph as the back of a game
box. On theme, and forces brevity. Keep the evidence table as the graded part.

# Criterion B: Developing ideas

## Bi (1 class)

> Develops detailed design specifications, which explain the success criteria for the design of a solution based on the analysis of the research.

This is the most important single page in the unit, because Dii tests against it
line by line. Time spent here is repaid twice.

**Deliverable.** 8 to 12 specifications. Every one has four parts:

| # | Specification | Measurable success criterion | How it will be tested | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Plays in one lunch break | Setup to pack away under 25 minutes | Timed user trial, 3 groups | Aiii implication 2 |

**Required categories**, at least one each: audience fit, playtime, components and
materials, rules clarity, balance and fairness, aesthetics, accessibility, cost.

**Two hard rules.**
- If it cannot be measured, it is not a specification. "Fun" fails. "At least 7 of
  10 testers choose to play a second round" passes.
- The "how it will be tested" column is written now, not in Di. Di expands it into
  real instruments. Writing it now stops students from setting untestable specs.

## Bii (3 classes)

> Develops a range of feasible design ideas, using an appropriate medium(s) and detailed annotation, which can be correctly interpreted by others.

**Task, three parts.**

**1. Forced range: the mechanic draw.** Each student draws two family cards from
the catalog and must generate a concept using at least one mechanic from each. Draw
three times. This produces genuine range rather than six versions of one idea.
Across the full set, concepts must touch at least **three different families**.

**2. Six to eight annotated concepts**, on a standard A3 template so annotation has
somewhere to live. Each concept shows:
- A core loop diagram, what a player does on their turn, in order
- Board or component sketch
- Mechanics used, named from the catalog
- Annotation on how it serves the audience, referencing a Bi spec by number
- One risk: the part most likely to fail

Hand drawing is the better medium here, because MYP rewards annotation density and
students annotate more freely by hand. Digital allowed if annotation is as rich.

**3. Two paper prototypes, actually played for five minutes.** Feasibility is in
the descriptor. An idea nobody has tried is not demonstrably feasible.

**The interpretation test, which is also the formative.** Swap sheets with a
classmate. They explain your concept back to you from the sheet alone, with no
talking from you. You write down what they got wrong. That list is submitted with
the concepts and is direct evidence for "can be correctly interpreted by others",
which is otherwise very hard to evidence.

**Class shape.** Class 1 mechanic draw and rapid concepts. Class 2 develop and
annotate the best six. Class 3 paper prototypes and the interpretation test.

## Biii (2 classes)

> Presents the chosen design and justifies fully and critically its selection with detailed reference to the design specification.

This is the strand where students write "I chose this one because it is the best".
The fix is to make the case against the winner compulsory.

**Task.** Each student pitches their strongest concept to their group. The group
converges on one direction. Then each student **individually** writes the
justification, including students whose concept was not chosen.

**Deliverable, two parts.**

**1. Weighted decision matrix.** Three finalist concepts scored against every Bi
specification, weights assigned and justified.

**2. Critical justification, one to two pages**, with four required sections:
- Why the chosen design wins, referencing specs by number
- **The case against it.** What it does worse than the concepts rejected. This is
  the section that converts "justifies" into "justifies critically".
- What was carried over from the rejected concepts into the final
- What is still unresolved and how the build will resolve it

**Why this works with groups.** A student who lost the vote writing an honest
critical justification of someone else's concept produces excellent evidence. Say
so openly, so it does not feel like a punishment.

## Biv (3 classes)

> Develops accurate and detailed planning drawings/diagrams and outlines requirements for the creation of the chosen solution.

Underused in tabletop units and genuinely rich. "Planning drawings" is not one
drawing, it is a production pack.

**Deliverable: Production Pack, 4 to 8 pages.**

| Page | Content |
|---|---|
| Component manifest | Every component, quantity, size in mm, material, who makes it |
| Board drawing | To scale, dimensioned, with a title block |
| Card template | Dimensioned, showing bleed, safe margin, and type sizes |
| Token or piece drawings | Orthographic, or a flat pattern if folded |
| Iconography sheet | Every icon drawn at final size, with its meaning |
| Rulebook map | Section structure and page order, not the prose |
| Requirements | Materials, tools, quantities, cost, time per component |

**Individual portion.** Each student draws the components they will personally make
in C, so Biv and Ci name the same owner.

**Skills teaching needed.** Scale, dimensioning conventions, and title blocks. Most
Grade 9s have met these in G8 bridge or 3D modelling; expect one reteach class.

# Criterion C: Creating the solution

## Ci (1 class)

> Constructs a detailed and logical plan, which describes the efficient use of time and resources, sufficient for peers to be able to follow to create the solution.

**Deliverable, two linked plans.**

**1. Group plan.** Gantt chart or task board over the 10 build classes: task, owner,
dependency, time estimate, resources, with the **critical path marked**.

**2. Personal task plan.** Each student's own components, class by class, with a
fallback if a resource is unavailable.

**The peer test, which is the descriptor.** "Sufficient for peers to follow" is
testable, so test it. Swap plans with another group. They read yours for five
minutes and then narrate what they would do in build class 1 and class 4. Anything
they cannot answer is a gap. Record the gaps and revise. Submit both versions.

## Cii (during the build block)

> Demonstrates excellent technical skills when making the solution.

The hardest strand to evidence individually inside a group build. Proposal:
**technical skill badges**, evidenced in a making log.

**Four skill tracks**, matched to the kit actually available. Technical skill in
this unit is not only fabrication: designing a rule set that works is a technical
skill, and so is laying out a card a nine year old can read across a table.

| Track | Skills | Kit |
|---|---|---|
| Systems and rules design | Rules that survive a cold read, balancing with numbers, icon language design, teaching the game in under 5 minutes | None |
| Graphic design | Card layout with bleed and safe margin, typography for audience and table distance, colour and contrast including colour blind safe choices, export ready artwork | Colour printers, print shop |
| Digital fabrication | 3D modelling tokens, miniatures, trays and inserts; printing well (orientation, supports, tolerance, fit); laser etched markings; designing a part reproducible 20 times | 3D printers, small laser etcher |
| Hand fabrication and finishing | Precision cutting, scoring and folding; mounting, hinging and board construction; surface finish, edges and corners; jig making for repeatable parts | Art and craft supplies |

**Kit notes.** The large laser cutter is unreliable, so nothing should depend on
it without a stated fallback. The small etcher and the 3D printers are the
dependable digital routes. Print shop orders cost money and need lead time, so
anything going to the shop must be finalised earlier than everything else, and
that decision belongs on the Biv requirements page.

**Requirement.** Each student demonstrates **three skills at depth, across at
least two tracks**. Three done properly beats ten touched lightly, and the two
track minimum stops a student spending ten classes at a 3D printer and calling it
a build. For each, three things: a dated photo of work in progress with their initials or
hand visible on the part, a photo of the outcome, and a note. The note covers what
went wrong, what they changed, and what the second attempt did better.

**Why this shape.** Excellent skill needs something to be excellent against. One
clean photograph shows a good outcome, not skill. A poor first attempt beside a
fixed second one shows both.

## Ciii (during the build block)

> Follows the plan to create the solution, which functions as intended and is presented appropriately.

**Deliverable.** The finished game, complete: all components, a rulebook of **no
more than 4 pages**, and a box or storage solution. Presented appropriately means
it survives being handed to a stranger. Hand students
[OnePageRules](https://onepagerules.com/) as the model; structure, iconography and
a worked example beat prose every time.

**The cold read test.** A group who has never seen the game receives the box and the
rulebook and nothing else. They set up and play with no help. Students observe in
silence and record every question asked and every rule misread. This is evidence
for Ciii, and it is the rehearsal for Di.

## Civ (ongoing, logged weekly)

> Fully justifies changes made to the chosen design and plan when making the solution.

**Deliverable.** Change Log, dated, one row per change:

| Date | What changed | What triggered it | Options considered | Why this option | Effect on spec or plan |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

**The one thing that makes or breaks this strand.** Students write the whole log the
night before it is due, and it shows. Build in a **five minute log stop** at the end
of every build class. Entries must be dated and the dates must be spread. Say plainly
that an undated log written in one sitting cannot reach 7-8.

**Prompt for a good entry.** A change with no trigger is a whim. A change with no
options considered is a reaction. 7-8 needs both.

# Criterion D: Evaluating

## Di (2 classes)

> Designs detailed and relevant testing methods, which generate data, to measure the success of the solution.

Methods drawn from DP A2.1 user centred research: user observation, interviews,
focus groups, questionnaires, user trials, personae and scenarios.

**Deliverable: Test Plan, with the instruments actually built.** Naming a method is
not designing one. Each test needs:

| Which spec | Method | Participants | Data type | Instrument | Success threshold |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Then the instruments themselves, attached:
- The questionnaire, with its actual questions
- The observation sheet, with what is being tallied
- The interview script, with its prompts
- The user trial protocol, with what is timed or counted

**Teach the distinction explicitly.** Qualitative data tells you why, quantitative
tells you how much. A 7-8 test plan uses both and knows which spec needs which.

**Match the method to the spec.** Playtime needs a stopwatch, not an opinion. Rules
clarity needs observation of misplays, not a question asking if the rules were clear.
Students will default to questionnaires for everything. This is the thing to correct.

## Dii (2 classes)

> Critically evaluates the success of the solution against the design specification based on authentic product testing.

**Class 1: the real session.** Target audience plays. Every student runs their own
instrument from Di and collects their own data.

**Class 2: the evaluation.** Point by point against every Bi specification:

| Spec | Test used | Data collected | Met, partly met, not met | Evidence | What this means |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

**Where "critically" lives.** A closing section on the limits of their own evidence:
sample size, one session only, testers who knew the designers, the difference between
what people said and what they did. A student who reports a spec as met on the basis
of three testers and says so is showing more judgement than one who claims certainty.

## Diii (1 class)

> Explains how the solution could be improved.

**Deliverable.** Ranked improvements, each traced to evidence:

| Improvement | Which spec it addresses | Dii evidence that prompted it | Why this would work | Cost and feasibility | Rank |

**Two rules.**
- Every improvement traces to a specific Dii finding. An improvement with no
  evidence behind it is a preference.
- Include **one improvement for something that passed**. Meeting a spec is not the
  same as being as good as it could be, and noticing that is a 7-8 move.

## Div (2 classes)

> Explains the impact of the product on the client/target audience.

You were unsure here, and the reason is that impact is easy to confuse with
satisfaction. "They enjoyed it" is a Dii finding. Impact asks what the product does
to the audience and to others around them.

**Class 1: the client session.** Each team takes its game to **its own client
group**. This is not a mass invite event; the audience is the specific group from
Ai, which is what makes the impact evidence honest. A broader whole-school
showcase can follow afterwards, but Div is assessed on the client session.

**Class 2: the impact report.** Individual, delivered as an A2 poster with a three
minute pitch, or a two page report if you prefer to grade on paper.

**Required sections**, which are what push past "they liked it":
1. **Who was affected**, including people beyond the players
2. **Intended impact against actual impact**, quoting the Ai need statement directly
3. **Evidence**, drawn from the showcase: quotes, observation tallies, what people did
4. **Unintended effects**, positive and negative. Did quieter students engage more?
   Did anyone feel excluded by the reading level, the language, the hand dexterity
   required?
5. **Wider impact**, at least two of: the school, families, materials and waste,
   cost, cultural appropriateness for a multilingual audience
6. **Was the original need met**, answered plainly, yes, partly or no, with reasons

**The bookend.** Quoting their own Ai need statement closes a loop that has been
open for 36 classes. It is where the strongest students show they understood the
whole arc.

# Practice material and formative assessment

Formatives are ungraded unless marked otherwise. Each is small enough to sit inside
the class that precedes the strand it prepares for.

## Criterion A

| For | Formative | Time | What it fixes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ai | **Need or want sort.** 12 statement cards about a demographic, sort into need, want, and assumption. | 15 min | Students assert wants as needs |
| Ai | **Weak brief autopsy.** A deliberately vague need statement written by you. In pairs, find the three unjustified claims. | 15 min | Shows what justification looks like by its absence |
| Aii | **Spend the tokens.** Practice run of the research budget on a fake brief before doing it for real. | 20 min | Prioritisation without the stakes |
| Aii | **Question quality ladder.** Rank six research questions from useless to sharp. | 10 min | "Do people like games?" versus "How long will a Grade 6 sit at one table?" |
| Aiii | **Teardown together.** You lead a full teardown of one game on the board, thinking aloud. | 1 class portion | Models the depth expected |
| Aiii | **Mechanic spotting speed run.** Play 10 minutes of a game, list every catalog mechanic you can name. | 20 min | Builds the shared vocabulary |
| Aiv | **Brief versus spec sort.** 10 statements, decide which belong in Aiv and which in Bi. | 10 min | The single most common A to B confusion |

## Criterion B

| For | Formative | Time | What it fixes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bi | **Make it measurable.** 8 unmeasurable specs, rewrite each with a number and a test. | 20 min | "Fun", "easy", "attractive" |
| Bi | **Spec to test matching.** Given a spec, choose the method that could actually test it. | 15 min | Sets up Di early |
| Bii | **Two family challenge.** Draw two catalog families, sketch a concept in 8 minutes. Repeat three times. | 30 min | Fluency and range before it counts |
| Bii | **Annotation density check.** Compare two sketches of the same idea, one bare and one annotated. Count what you can only learn from the annotated one. | 15 min | Annotation is the assessed part, not the drawing |
| Bii | **Interpretation rehearsal.** Explain a partner's sketch back to them, first run, ungraded. | 15 min | Reveals what a reader actually needs |
| Biii | **Matrix with a rigged winner.** Give a matrix where the highest scoring option is obviously wrong. Discuss what the matrix missed. | 20 min | Stops blind trust in the numbers |
| Biii | **Write the case against.** Practise arguing against a design you like. | 15 min | The hardest half of Biii |
| Biv | **Dimension a card.** Take a real card, measure it, draw it to scale with bleed and margin marked. | 25 min | Scale and dimensioning conventions |
| Biv | **Manifest a real game.** Open a published game, write its full component manifest. | 25 min | Shows how much detail a manifest carries |

## Criterion C

| For | Formative | Time | What it fixes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ci | **Follow a bad plan.** Give a vague plan, ask students to state exactly what they would do first. They cannot. | 15 min | Makes "sufficient for peers to follow" concrete |
| Ci | **Critical path hunt.** Given 10 tasks with dependencies, find what blocks everything. | 20 min | Sequencing, not just listing |
| Cii | **Skill clinics.** Short rotating stations before the build starts: cutting and scoring, bleed setup, corner rounding, hinging. | 1 class | Skill before it is assessed |
| Cii | **The good failure.** Show a first attempt and a fixed second attempt of your own. | 10 min | Normalises documenting failure |
| Civ | **Log stop.** Five minutes at the end of every build class, written in place. | 5 min x 10 | The single highest value routine in the unit |
| Civ | **Trigger, options, choice.** Rewrite three weak log entries to include all three. | 15 min | Turns a diary into a justification |
| Ciii | **Rulebook cold read, internal.** Swap draft rulebooks between groups a week before the deadline. | 30 min | Finds rule gaps while there is still time |

## Criterion D

| For | Formative | Time | What it fixes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Di | **Method matching.** Six specs, choose the right method for each and say why. | 20 min | The questionnaire-for-everything reflex |
| Di | **Write five bad questions.** Leading, double barrelled, vague. Then fix them. | 20 min | Instrument quality |
| Di | **Observation sheet build.** Watch a 5 minute play clip, tally misplays using a sheet you designed. | 25 min | Observation generates data too |
| Dii | **Met, partly met, not met.** Given data and a spec, decide and defend. | 15 min | Students over-claim "met" |
| Dii | **Limits of the evidence.** Given a result from 3 testers, list four reasons to be cautious. | 15 min | Where "critically" lives |
| Diii | **Trace the improvement.** Given a Dii table, propose improvements and mark which have no evidence. | 20 min | Preference versus finding |
| Div | **Satisfaction versus impact sort.** 10 statements, sort into "they enjoyed it" and "it changed something". | 15 min | The core confusion in Div |
| Div | **Unintended effects hunt.** For a familiar product, list three effects the designer did not intend. | 15 min | Section 4 of the report |
| Div | **Pitch rehearsal.** Three minute run in pairs before the showcase. | 20 min | Poster and pitch quality |

# Reusable class routines

- **Log stop.** Last five minutes of every build class, Civ entry written in place.
- **Catalog callout.** Whenever a mechanic comes up in discussion, name its family
  aloud. Builds shared vocabulary at no time cost.
- **Spec check.** Open the Bi page at the start of every B and C class. Specs that
  are never reread do not get met.
- **Swap and read.** Any deliverable claiming to be readable by others gets read by
  others before submission. Applies to Bii, Ci, Ciii.
