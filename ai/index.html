What AI is now

A lot of people tried a free chatbot in early 2023, watched it make something up, and decided that AI could not be trusted. The tools have changed a great deal since then. This page explains how they work now, what they still get wrong, and how to tell the difference.

Wikipedia, then AI

For about ten years, many teachers told students not to use Wikipedia, because anyone could edit it. That was true, and it was a real reason to be careful. It was also an incomplete picture. Wikipedia records every edit, asks for a source on every claim, and usually repairs damage within minutes. Something similar is happening now with AI.

Where a model's knowledge stops

A model learns from text collected up to a certain date, called its training cutoff. On its own, it knows nothing that happened after that date. Some tools can search the web while they answer, which is like opening a window in that wall. Many tools still cannot, and a tool that can may not do it for every question.

5 of 9

How a model writes

A language model does not look up an answer. It predicts the next word from everything it has read, then the next word after that. This is why its writing sounds fluent, and it is also why it can be fluently wrong. A made-up book title is simply a likely sequence of words.

6 of 9

Pages that pretend to be new

When a tool searches, it reads the same web you do. A growing share of that web was written by AI, quickly, to look recent. A recipe that says it was updated last week may have changed only its date. That works because we all have a recency bias. Search engines and AI tools both prefer recent pages, so the trick works on both of them.

Which model

There are many AI tools, from many countries, and they differ in ways that matter: whether they can search, whether anyone can run them on their own computer, and where the text you type ends up.
