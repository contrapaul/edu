What AI is now

A lot of people tried a free chatbot in early 2023, watched it make something up, and decided that AI could not be trusted. The tools have changed a great deal since then. This page explains how they work now, what they still get wrong, and how to tell the difference.

Wikipedia, then AI

For about ten years, many teachers told students not to use Wikipedia, because anyone could edit it. That was true, and it was a real reason to be careful. It was also an incomplete picture. Wikipedia records every edit, asks for a source on every claim, and usually repairs damage within minutes. Students who were told to avoid it used it anyway, and nobody showed them how to check an article properly. Something similar is happening now with AI.

Wikipedia, around 2008

What teachers said

  • Anyone can change an article.
  • You can't tell who wrote it.
  • It might be wrong, so don't use it.

What was already true

  • Every edit is recorded, with a history anyone can read.
  • Every claim is supposed to have a footnote to a source.
  • Bad edits are usually undone within minutes.

AI chatbots, since 2023

What teachers say

  • It makes things up.
  • It can't see anything after 2021.
  • It might be wrong, so don't use it.

What is true now

  • Many tools search the web while they answer and show the pages they read.
  • You can open the links and check them yourself.
  • The tools still make mistakes, but the mistakes have changed. The rest of this page is about them.

The useful lesson about Wikipedia was never to avoid it. It was to read the sources an article cites, look at its history, and treat it as a place to start. The same approach works with AI, once you know what to look for.

The same five questions, three years apart

In March 2023 a teacher asked a free chatbot to suggest sources for a report on Hadrian's Wall, and then asked it four more questions. In September 2026 he asked a free chatbot the same five questions. Both conversations are shown below exactly as they happened. The coloured underlines were added afterwards, when each claim was checked. Red means the claim was invented, amber means it should be checked, and green means it matched the source.

  1. 01

    In 2023 the model produced book titles that sounded right. Two of the four books do not exist. In 2026 every source it gave was a real page with a working link, because the model searched the web before it answered.

  2. 02

    The mistakes did not go away. They changed. The 2026 model wrote that English Heritage "specifically notes that he never returned to Britain" and linked to a page. That page does not say it. In 2023 the model invented a book. In 2026 it invented what a real page says.

  3. 03

    The 2026 answer about the Antonine Wall has no sources at all. The same model, in the same conversation, answered that question from memory instead of searching. The only sign was that the links were missing. A tool that can search does not search every time.

Where a model's knowledge stops

A model learns from text collected up to a certain date, called its training cutoff. On its own, it knows nothing that happened after that date. Some tools can search the web while they answer, which is like opening a window in that wall. Many tools still cannot, and a tool that can may not do it for every question.

How a model writes

A language model does not look up an answer. It predicts the next word from everything it has read, then the next word after that. This is why its writing sounds fluent, and it is also why it can be fluently wrong. A made-up book title is simply a likely sequence of words.

Pages that pretend to be new

When a tool searches, it reads the same web you do. A growing share of that web was written by AI, quickly, to look recent. A recipe that says it was updated last week may have changed only its date. Search engines and AI tools both prefer recent pages, so the trick works on both of them.
