The internal assessment is a 50-hour design project. You identify an existing product, research the person who uses it, analyse where it fails them, then develop and present a redesigned solution supported by a physical model.

From the guide
The task

"The design project is an open-ended task in which a student must identify, analyse, evaluate and redesign an existing product to meet the needs of an intended user(s). As part of the project, students need to develop a physical fidelity model for testing and evaluation purposes."

Design technology guide, p. 67.

How to Read This Guide

Three different kinds of information appear on this page, and it matters that you can tell them apart.

  • From the guide. Quoted directly from the IB guide, with a page reference. This is what you are actually assessed on.
  • Mr. K's method. A way of meeting the requirement. Useful, tested, and not the only way. Every template on this page is my method.
  • Mr. K's commentary. My opinion or judgment call, including things specific to our school and context.

Where this guide says "must", check which of the three it is coming from. If a requirement is not quoted with a page reference, treat it as advice rather than as a rule.

Key Rules

Every rule below is stated in the guide. Page references are given so you can check them.

  • Maximum 35 A4 pages. The examiner stops reading once the page or word limit is reached. (p. 66)
  • Maximum 3,500 words, excluding annotations of 10 words or fewer. (p. 66)
  • Annotations must be no more than 10 words each. Anything longer counts toward the word count. (p. 66)
  • A4 portrait layout throughout, except the detailed dimension drawings in criterion D. (p. 66)
  • You redesign an existing product. You do not invent a new one. (p. 67)
  • You must develop a physical fidelity model for testing and evaluation. (p. 67)
  • Required evidence includes digital images of your primary persona showing their issues with an existing product. (p. 73)
  • Reference sources as footnotes on the page. Footnotes do not count toward the word count, and no separate references page is required. (p. 72)
  • Appendices are not read by examiners. Anything critical to assessment must be in the body of the report. (pp. 66, 68)
  • Annotations must be legible, preferably typed. Handwritten annotations must be equivalent to Arial 11pt, and black ink is recommended. (p. 66)
  • Subject-specific vocabulary is rewarded across all of the assessment criteria. (p. 67)
Critical rule
Redesign, do not invent

The task is to redesign a product that already exists. Every criterion after A refers back to that product: criterion B defines the problem with it, criterion E compares your solution against it. A project that invents a new product instead of redesigning the one your research identified leaves criterion E with nothing to compare against, and the criteria have no way to reward it.

From the guide
Choosing a project

"The design project should demonstrate a level of complexity that aligns with the expectations of a Diploma Programme (DP) course."

"It is important to note that the selected product being redesigned should correspond to the complexity and appropriateness of the project. It is unlikely for a student to achieve higher bands on the assessment criteria by attempting to redesign a simple problem with an overly complex solution."

Design technology guide, p. 66.

Mr. K's commentary
Proportion between problem and solution

Read that second quote carefully, because it cuts both ways. An ambitious solution bolted onto a trivial problem will not lift your marks. Neither will a genuinely complex problem addressed with something you could buy for five yuan. The problem and the solution need to be proportionate to each other, and both need to be worth a DP course. Come talk to me before you commit to a product.

Mr. K's commentary
What the two right-hand columns tell you

Criterion A gets nine pages and only 900 words. It is carried by images, storyboards and annotations, not by paragraphs. Criterion B gets four pages and 1,200 words, the largest word allocation on the project in the smallest page allocation. That is where sustained writing belongs. Criteria D and E together get fourteen pages and 500 words, so they are almost entirely drawings, photographs and annotations.

Students who write essays in A and bullet points in B are working against both allocations at once.

Watch out
On the word count

The guide describes the word count in two ways that pull against each other. On p. 72 it calls the count "a recommendation only, not a target" and says students and teachers have flexibility to allocate it based on assessment needs. On p. 66 it states that teachers must not award marks for work over the word or page limit, and that the examiner will stop reading once these limits are reached.

Treat the per-criterion figures as guidance for how to distribute your writing, and treat 3,500 words and 35 pages as hard ceilings.

Referencing

From the guide
Referencing and academic integrity

"Students must ensure their assessment work adheres to the IB's academic integrity policy and that all sources are appropriately referenced and presented on the page as a footnote. Footnote references do not count towards the word count. No additional references page is required."

Design technology guide, p. 72.

Footnotes are free. A bibliography page is not, because it consumes one of your 35 pages and the examiner does not need it. Reference on the page where you use the source.

Empathize is the foundation of the project. You research a primary persona, analyse the specific task where an existing product fails them, and evaluate a range of existing products. Every later criterion refers back to what you establish here.

From the guide
What this criterion assesses

"This criterion assesses the student's ability to:

  • analyse a primary persona appropriate to the redesign of the product
  • present a storyboard based on the observations of a primary persona using a product for a specific task
  • analyse existing products and the successes and weaknesses of key features."

Design technology guide, p. 72.

Mr. K's commentary
What actually moves between the bands

Read the three descriptors down the column rather than across. The content stays the same at every band. What changes is the verb.

  • Persona: states, then describes, then explains. Explaining means giving the reasons behind the persona's needs, not more detail about them.
  • Storyboard: a limited task analysis, then a task analysis, then a comprehensive task analysis. The unit being judged is the task analysis, not the drawing quality.
  • Products: outlines a list of key features, then describes successes and weaknesses of some key features, then evaluates accurately across all key features.

That last one is the most common place marks are lost in this criterion. A list of features is a 1 to 3. Judging them is a 7 to 9.

From the guide
Required evidence

"The required evidence for this strand is:

  • a range of digital images of the primary persona displaying their issues with an existing product
  • an explanation of the characteristics, context, preferences, motivations and requirements of the primary persona."

Design technology guide, p. 73.

Does the Persona Have to Be a Real Person?

The guide does not say. You will hear this claimed confidently in both directions, so it is worth knowing exactly what the source does and does not contain.

Pointing toward a composite. The glossary defines a primary persona as "a profile of the user for a product" that provides "a human face and story to a particular audience" (p. 68). The guide also states that the individual or group directly using a product "may also be categorized as a primary persona" (p. 69). A group cannot be one named individual.

Pointing toward a specific person. The required evidence above asks for digital images of the primary persona displaying their issues with an existing product. The storyboard strand is assessed on "the observations of a primary persona using a product" (p. 72). Both are difficult to produce for someone who does not exist.

Nothing in the internal assessment section requires the persona to be real, named, interviewed, or a single individual. Equally, nothing permits a persona invented from imagination, because that persona cannot supply the required images.

Mr. K's method
Work with someone you can photograph

Work with an actual person you can photograph and observe using the product. Not because the guide names that as a requirement, but because the evidence it does require is hard to generate any other way.

Interviews, surveys and observations are listed as methods you may use (p. 69), so choose whichever produce the characteristics, context, preferences, motivations and requirements you need to explain. If your persona draws on several real users, say so and say which parts came from where. That is honest, it is defensible, and it still gives you a person to photograph.

From the guide
What to research

"The student must research the primary persona's characteristics, context, preferences, motivations and requirements to gain insights into their needs. This research should include but is not limited to:

  • demographic considerations such as age, education, ethnicity, access challenges, family status, and occupation
  • abilities and skills
  • behaviour patterns and attitudes
  • goals, concerns, needs and opportunities
  • the primary persona's physical, social and technological environment."

Design technology guide, p. 69.

Those five research areas and the five explanation points in the required evidence are your structure. Cover all of them. The template below arranges them in an order that reads well and leaves room for the images.

Mr. K's method
Human factors

Anthropometric data, biomechanics and percentile ranges are not named anywhere in the internal assessment section of the guide, so do not let anyone tell you they are required here. I still want you collecting them.

The reason is p. 67: subject-specific vocabulary "is rewarded across all of the assessment criteria". A persona whose hand span you have measured against percentile data produces specification points in criterion B that you can actually test in criterion D. A persona described only in adjectives does not. Human factors are how a design technology student explains a requirement rather than asserting it.

Add these under CHARACTERISTICS and REQUIREMENTS where they are relevant to your product:

  • Anthropometric data: relevant body dimensions and the percentile range you are designing for
  • Biomechanical considerations: grip force, reach range, range of motion
  • Ergonomic requirements: posture, repetitive strain risk, duration of use
Mr. K's commentary
A note on secondary personas

While all the IB commissioned sample IA papers include a secondary persona, there is zero reference to "secondary personas" in the IA material. There is a reference to personae being created following primary and secondary research tucked into page 65 of the Teacher Support Materials document, but there is no discussion of primary vs. secondary. It is my opinion that the secondary personae in the sample IA documents is either a residual element from older papers or possibly a requirement that was provided when creating the samples but later removed from the curriculum. As a result, I'm instructing my students to only create a primary persona, and would encourage other teachers to do the same.

Common problems
Where marks go missing in this strand
  • No digital images of the persona showing their issues with the existing product. This is required evidence, and its absence caps the strand.
  • The persona is described rather than explained. Description is the middle band. Explanation means giving the reasons behind the needs.
  • One or more of characteristics, context, preferences, motivations and requirements is missing. All five are named in the required evidence.
  • Frustrations are vague ("it is uncomfortable") rather than tied to a specific moment of product use.
  • No link between the persona's requirements and the design of the existing product.
  • The persona runs past two pages, squeezing the storyboard and product analysis.

Page allocationApproximately three A4 pages or the equivalent.

From the guide
Required evidence

"The required evidence for this strand is:

  • a storyboard for the chosen primary persona that highlights the issues of using a product."

"Note that:

  • extended writing to address this strand should not be included."

Design technology guide, p. 73.

Critical rule
No extended writing

This is the instruction students most often miss. Three pages of storyboard with paragraphs of explanation underneath is the wrong shape. Communicate visually, with short annotations. Your 900 words for criterion A are needed elsewhere.

The Storyboard Is a Task Analysis

From the guide
Task analysis

"Task analysis: A strategy used to develop empathy and understanding of how users perform a task to achieve their intended goal."

Design technology guide, p. 68.

Every band descriptor for this strand judges the task analysis: limited, then satisfactory, then comprehensive. The drawing is the medium. The task analysis is the assessed content. Criterion B then requires a problem statement "informed through the task analysis", so this strand is also where your next criterion comes from.

From the guide
What a storyboard does

"Storyboards are created as a result of the student's qualitative and quantitative research into the primary persona or intended user(s). They are used to communicate visually the sequential stages of the intended user(s) completing a task analysis with an identified product and the challenges they encounter. A storyboard can help students map out opportunities to redesign a product to provide a solution."

Design technology guide, p. 69.

Mr. K's method
One task, not one day

Pick one task, not one day. "Braking while carrying a bag" is a task. "A morning commute" is a lifestyle. The narrower the task, the more comprehensive your analysis of it can be inside three pages.

Break the task into its sequential stages, one frame per stage, and let the frames run all the way through to the point of failure and its consequence. Annotate each frame with what the persona is doing and what the issue is. Keep every annotation to 10 words or fewer so none of it touches your word count.

Mr. K's storyboard structure

STORYBOARD: [Name of the single task]
Persona: [Name]        Existing product: [Product name]

Visual sequence, one frame per stage of the task.
Annotations only. No paragraphs. 10 words or fewer per annotation.

FRAME 1  [Image]  Annotation: [Action at this stage]
FRAME 2  [Image]  Annotation: [Action, and the issue emerging]
FRAME 3  [Image]  Annotation: [The point of failure]
FRAME 4  [Image]  Annotation: [Consequence for the persona]
FRAME 5  [Image]  Annotation: [What they do instead, or give up on]
FRAME 6  [Image]  Annotation: [End state of the task]

Optional final frame:
- A short annotated summary of the opportunity this reveals.
- Keep it to annotations. This strand takes no extended writing.
Common problems
Where marks go missing in this strand
  • Extended writing included, which the guide explicitly says to leave out.
  • A general story about the persona's life rather than a sequential analysis of one task.
  • Frames show what happens but never indicate the issue, so no task analysis is visible to assess.
  • The task is too broad to analyse comprehensively in three pages.
  • The storyboard is not linked to the task analysis that criterion B then depends on.
  • Images with no annotations at all.

From the guide
Required evidence

"The required evidence for this strand is:

  • annotated images (see "Features of the design project" section) of the product testing. A typical range should be between four to six products."

Design technology guide, p. 73.

From the guide
Existing products

"Students should conduct functional analysis of existing, similar products. In cases where a product is not available for testing and analysis, a digital product may be used, excluding the user interface (UI) and user experience (UX). This analysis enables students to scrutinize what makes existing product designs a success or failure."

"A student may make a comparative model that includes:

  • the success of a range of products' form and functionality
  • materials and components
  • aesthetics and ergonomic features
  • manufacturing and construction techniques
  • environmental and safety considerations."

Design technology guide, p. 69.

Watch out
This strand is about testing

The required evidence is annotated images of product testing, not of products. Wherever you can, get the product in your hands and test it against the task from your storyboard. The guide only permits a digital product when one is not available for testing, and even then the UI and UX are excluded.

Mr. K's commentary
Where to source products

For products you cannot get hold of, I'm strongly encouraging my students to use retailers and product information relevant to them. In practice this means Taobao and JD.com rather than Amazon, Shopee, eBay, or any other site not operating inside China. Use local currency, metric measurements, and products that are actually available to you.

This is more work on the front end because it requires moving beyond a quick Google search, and it is far better for comparison and for understanding how features are marketed. It also has a practical benefit for this strand: a product you can order is a product you can test, and testing is what the required evidence asks for.

Capture UX Here

Criterion B requires design specifications with reference to research, specifically "UI and UX derived from existing product analysis in the empathize phase" (p. 74). That analysis happens in this strand. If you do not record user experience observations while testing these products, criterion B has nothing to draw on.

Mr. K's method
Same headings, every product

Cover all key features of each product, because the top band says all. Work through the same headings for every product in the same order so the comparison is easy to follow and easy to mark. Finish each analysis with a judgment, not a summary. The verb in the top band is "evaluates".

Common problems
Where marks go missing in this strand
  • Fewer than four products analysed, when the guide gives a typical range of four to six.
  • Products described rather than evaluated. Description sits in the middle band.
  • Only some key features are judged, when the top band asks for all of them.
  • Images of products rather than annotated images of product testing.
  • Weak choice of products, especially where obvious comparators are skipped.
  • Inconsistent details between products, such as metric for one and imperial for another, or prices from different markets.
  • No link between the products analysed and the persona's requirements.
  • No user experience observations recorded, leaving criterion B without the UX evidence it requires.

Criterion B turns your research into a defined project. It has two parts: a problem statement drawn from the task analysis, and a design specification whose requirements refer back to what your research found. This is the smallest page allocation on the project and the largest word allocation.

From the guide
What this criterion assesses

"This criterion assesses the student's ability to:

  • produce a problem statement that identifies opportunities for the redesign of the product through the task analysis
  • produce product design specifications with reference to research (UI and UX derived from existing product analysis in the empathize phase)."

Mr. K's commentary
What actually moves between the bands
  • Problem statement: presents, then describes, then explains. Alongside that, the opportunities go from limited, to some, to key, and the link to the task analysis goes from superficial to informed through it.
  • Specification: the requirements are stated, then outlined, then explained. The reference to research goes from limited, to relevant, to relevant and detailed.

Both strands land on the same two ideas: explain rather than assert, and show the research behind each claim. The phrase "informed through the task analysis" appears in the two upper bands. If your problem statement does not visibly come out of your storyboard, you are capped at the bottom band no matter how well written it is.

Required evidence

"The required evidence for this strand is:

  • a problem statement, which includes:
    • the current situation
    • the current problem

Design technology guide, p. 74.

From the guide
The problem statement

"Students should produce a concise problem statement that sets the direction of the design project intentions. The problem statement should incorporate the intended persona's user needs for the chosen product and students should be open to refining the problem statement based on new insights, feedback and challenges."

Design technology guide, p. 70.

Three components, all required, on roughly one page. The third is the one most often left out: a summary of the research that shows this is the right problem to be solving. Without it there is nothing for the examiner to judge "appropriateness" against.

Note also that the guide expects the statement to be refined as the project develops. It is not a commitment you make once in September and never revisit.

Mr. K's problem statement template

PROBLEM STATEMENT

[What the situation is now, before any judgment about it.]

- From the persona: [Requirement or motivation that makes this problem matter]
- From the task analysis: [The stage where the product fails, and its effect]
- From the product analysis: [Which existing products fail this need, and how]
- Why this is the appropriate problem to address:

- [Key opportunity 1, traced to a stage of the task analysis]
- [Key opportunity 2, traced to a stage of the task analysis]
- [Key opportunity 3, traced to a stage of the task analysis]
Mr. K's method
List the opportunities explicitly

Write the opportunities as a short list at the end. The descriptors judge whether your statement identifies opportunities and whether they are informed through the task analysis, so making them explicit and traceable does you a favour. Naming the storyboard frame each one comes from takes four words and settles the question.

Common problems
Where marks go missing in this strand
  • The summary of research is missing, so the appropriateness of the problem is unevidenced.
  • The link to the task analysis is superficial, which is the wording of the bottom band.
  • The statement describes a large social issue rather than the situation and problem for this persona and this product.
  • The existing product is not named.
  • No opportunities for redesign are identified, only a complaint about the product.
  • The statement is never revisited, even after later research changes what you know.

Required evidence

"The required evidence for this strand is:

  • a design specification that captures both essential and desirable requirements, ensuring that the redesigned product meets the needs of the intended user."

From the guide
The design specification

"A design specification is generated from research data through a comprehensive investigation of the design opportunity and through the formulation of the problem statement. Students should prioritize the design's essential and desirable specifications, and these should be justified with references to sources."

"The design specification for the solution must include UX derived from product analysis and the empathize phase. Additionally, it should encompass aspects of the design dependent on their suitability for the product and their design context. The following should be considered as part of the design specification process.

  • Attributes: Desired qualities or characteristics
  • Components: Individual parts or elements
  • Constraints: Limitations or restrictions
  • Environment: Context or surroundings
  • Functions: Intended operations or actions
  • User needs: Specific functionality, usability and performance requirements
  • Safety: Avoiding potential hazards, harm or injury, and legal requirements
  • Aesthetics: Visual appeal, colour, shape, texture and style
  • Manufacture requirements: Material selection, production techniques and assembly requirements"

"This list is not prescriptive or exhaustive."

Design technology guide, p. 70.

Critical rule
Every requirement refers to the research

The difference between the bottom and top bands of this strand is the phrase "with limited reference to the research" against "with relevant and detailed reference to the research". A specification point you cannot trace to something in criterion A is not evidence of research, whatever else it is. If you cannot find its source, either go and do the research or take the point out.

Mr. K's method
A source column and measurable targets

Two things I add that the guide does not require.

First, a source column. The descriptors ask for reference to the research, and a column naming the persona, storyboard frame or product analysis behind each point makes that reference impossible to miss.

Second, measurable targets wherever a requirement can carry one. "Comfortable" cannot be tested in criterion D. "Handle diameter 32 to 38mm" can. The guide does not use the word measurable, so this is my advice rather than a rule, but a specification you cannot test is a specification that gives you nothing to do in criteria C, D and E.

Mr. K's design specification table

DESIGN SPECIFICATION

Group your points under the guide's categories, using the ones that apply
to your product: attributes, components, constraints, environment, functions,
user needs, safety, aesthetics, manufacture requirements. UX is required.

# | Category | Requirement | Explanation, with reference to research | Source | Priority
--|----------|-------------|------------------------------------------|--------|---------
1 | User needs | [What the product must do or be, with a target where possible] | [What the research showed, and why this requirement follows from it] | [Persona / Storyboard frame / Product #] | Essential
2 | Attributes | Handle diameter 32 to 38mm | Persona hand breadth measured at 78mm, 50th percentile. Product 1 handle is 28mm, below the 5th percentile, and the persona reported grip fatigue after ten minutes. | Persona + Product 1 | Essential
3 | Functions | Operable with one hand only | Task analysis frames 3 and 4: the persona's other hand is holding the frame throughout this stage. | Storyboard, frames 3 to 4 | Essential
4 | UX | [What using it should feel like, and at which stage of the task] | [Drawn from testing existing products in the empathize phase] | Product analysis | Essential

Essential: the design fails if this is not met.
Desirable: the design is improved by this, but its absence is not a failure.
Common problems
Where marks go missing in this strand
  • Requirements are stated rather than explained, which is the bottom band.
  • No reference to the research behind each requirement.
  • No UX requirements, when the guide states the specification must include UX derived from product analysis and the empathize phase.
  • Points that cannot be traced to anything in criterion A, often aesthetics or cost assumptions carried in from outside the project.
  • Everything marked essential, when the guide asks you to prioritize essential and desirable.
  • Requirements phrased so loosely that criteria C, D and E cannot test against them.
  • The specification is written and then never used again.

Present three feasible redesign ideas, then evaluate them against your specification through testing and user feedback and identify the one you are taking forward. The second strand ends in an annotated presentation drawing of that chosen idea.

Mr. K's commentary
What actually moves between the bands

The first strand has a hard gate before any of the quality language applies. Fewer than three ideas is the bottom band. Not a deduction, the band itself. Three ideas is the entry price for 3 to 4 and above, and above that the difference is whether the ideas address some key features or all of them.

The second strand is a verb ladder again: states, then describes, then evaluates the difference between the ideas against the specifications. Note what all three bands hold constant: through testing and user feedback. An evaluation done at your desk with no testing and nobody consulted does not reach any band cleanly.

From the guide
Ideation

"Students should use appropriate informal or formal sketches or drawings and digital or physical fidelity modelling to communicate their ideas visually. As the ideas are developed and refined, their feasibility is determined against the design specifications, which help determine the appropriate aspects of each idea."

Note the second required evidence point, which is easy to overlook. This strand is not a gallery of three finished concepts. It should show additional research and feedback feeding back into the ideas as they develop. Sketch, get a reaction, change something, show the change.

Mr. K's method
Make the three genuinely different

Make the three ideas genuinely different from one another: different mechanism, configuration, material or interaction, rather than the same object in three colours. The guide asks for "three of the best possible" ideas and for a diverse set, and three variations on one concept gives your evaluation nothing to compare.

Address all key features in each idea, because the top band asks for all of them. Annotate every drawing at 10 words or fewer so the annotations stay outside your word count.

What actually moves between the bands
  • Fidelity models: the model partially, then satisfactorily, then thoroughly addresses the problem statement. The testing goes from limited, to outlined, to evaluated against all of the design specifications.
  • Drawings: limited, then adequate, then comprehensive detail.

The word "all" in the top band is doing real work. Testing three specification points thoroughly will not reach 5 to 6 if your specification has twelve. This is the moment your criterion B decisions come back to you: a specification full of untestable wishes makes this band unreachable.

A physical model is required

Mr. K's method
One structure, repeated per cycle

Document each cycle in the same short structure: what you built, which specification points you tested, how you tested them, what the result was, and what you changed as a consequence. Repeating one structure makes the cycle legible at a glance and keeps you inside 300 words for the whole criterion.

Test with the intended user, not only with yourself. Every band of this strand refers to the needs of the intended user, and the required evidence names intended user testing directly. Record results as measurements or quotes rather than impressions.

Mr. K's method
Bill of materials

A bill of materials is not mentioned anywhere in the guide, so treat it as optional. I ask for one because the test in this strand is whether a third-party manufacturer could build your design from what you have given them, and a parts table answers questions your drawings cannot: what each component is made of, how many are needed, and how each is produced.

If you include one, number the parts to match your exploded view. If you are short on pages, the drawings come first.

What actually moves between the bands

The first strand moves on annotation quality alone: limited, then adequate, then comprehensive annotations of the key features. The drawings or photographs are assumed. The annotations are what is marked.

The second strand moves on the identified problems. At 1 to 2 you compare. At 3 to 4 you compare and address some of the identified problems. At 5 to 6 you compare and comprehensively address them. "Identified problems" means the ones from your problem statement and task analysis, so this criterion is only as strong as criterion B was specific.

Watch out
The presentation event is not assessed

There is a real presentation in this stage, and you choose its form. The guide says the student may present digitally, as a slide show, verbally, online or in whatever suits their context, and that the student decides who the audience should be: the teacher, peers, or members of the wider community (p. 71). None of that is formally assessed. What is assessed is the two pages of annotated evidence that go into your report.

Mr. K's method
Make every annotation do two jobs

Two to three images per page, every key feature annotated, and each annotation naming the specification point it satisfies. Ten words is enough for "Textured grip, 35mm diameter, meets spec 2" and that annotation does two jobs at once.

Comprehensive means every key feature, not just the interesting ones. Work around the product systematically so nothing is skipped.

Critical rule
Compare against the original product

The comparison is with the specific product identified at the start of the project for redesign, the one your persona uses and your storyboard analyses. Not the market in general, and not an idealised version of the problem. If your project has drifted so far that the original product is no longer a meaningful comparison, that drift will surface here.

Three things to demonstrate, and the middle one is the most often skipped. Showing how the solution is intended to be used takes you back to the task from your storyboard and shows the same task performed with your redesign.

Mr. K's method
Density beats prose here

Side-by-side images and a comparison table are my way of covering all three points in two pages with 200 words. Neither is required. What they buy you is density: a table row can carry a requirement, the original's performance, your solution's performance, and the evidence, in less than a sentence of prose.

Be honest about the requirements you did not fully meet. Nothing in the descriptors penalises an unmet specification point that you acknowledge, and the top band asks you to address the identified problems comprehensively, which includes the ones still outstanding.
