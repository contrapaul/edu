# Parents Notes (draft)

Drop-in copy for the `parentsNote` field in games.js. Entries are in the same order as GAMES, so you can work top to bottom and paste each one straight into its game object.

A few things worth knowing before editing:

- The site currently renders `parentsNote` as a single plain-text paragraph (greatgames.js uses textContent), so if you want the bullets below to show up as real bullets on the page, that file needs a small tweak. Otherwise fold them into the paragraph or keep them here.
- Age ratings and content notes already live in games.js per game; these notes are written to sit alongside those rather than repeat them, but they do reference the rating where it helps make the case.
- Tone target: conversational, first person ("me to you"), frank about content without being preachy. No em dashes anywhere, per house style.

## Subnautica (`subnautica`)

If your child likes the idea of being stranded somewhere and having to figure it out, this is one of my favorite recommendations on the list. You crash-land on an alien ocean planet and spend your time exploring, scavenging, building a base, and slowly piecing together what happened here from logs you find along the way. There's no combat to speak of; the game rewards planning (what do I need before I go deeper?) over reflexes, which is exactly the kind of thinking I want my students doing in class. The story unfolds through discovery rather than cutscenes, so players end up reading and listening to a lot of English in context, and that's quietly one of the best language immersion experiences I know of. Every creature you meet has a role in an ecosystem, so there's real marine biology inspiration baked into the design. I rate it 10+ because some of the deeper creatures are genuinely scary and can kill you if you're careless; that tension is part of what makes it good. If your child gets spooked easily by dark water and big shadows, give them a heads up before they dive in.

**Notes and concerns:**
- Sea creatures attack and can be deadly; the deep-sea ones are designed to be terrifying.
- No online multiplayer or chat; it's a solo experience.
- Oxygen management creates real pressure, which younger players may find intense at times.

## Subnautica: Below Zero (`subnautica-below-zero`)

Below Zero works as a standalone, so you don't need to have played the first one. You play Robin, who goes looking for her sister in the arctic region of the same planet, and the game keeps everything that made Subnautica work (curiosity, base building, careful planning) while adding surface biomes above the water line. The story is more personal this time around, which gives players a stronger reason to keep exploring beyond just "what's down there?" It's still very much a thinking game: you manage oxygen and resources, design your bases, and decide how far into the unknown you're willing to go. For my students it doubles as a great listening exercise, since so much of the story comes through logs and dialogue in English. I rate it 10+ for the same reasons as the original; some creatures are scary and can kill you if you push too deep. If your child enjoyed the first game, this is an easy yes.

**Notes and concerns:**
- Same creature danger as the original, with a few new terrifying encounters.
- Solo game, no online chat or multiplayer.
- The story leans on mystery and loss; some players find the tone heavier than the first game.

## Subnautica 2 (`subnautica-2`)

Subnautica 2 is the first in the series you can play with friends, and that changes the experience more than you'd expect; exploring an alien ocean together is a lot of fun. It's also still being built. The game launched into Early Access on May 14, 2026 for PC and Xbox Series X/S, which means it's playable today but not finished: content, story, and polish are all still coming over the next couple of years. I'm recommending it anyway because what's there already is excellent, and my students who play it treat it like a shared project they're helping shape. The core loop (scan, craft, build, plan your next dive) is unchanged, so everything I said about the first two games still applies here. If you buy it now, know that the price goes up when Early Access ends, and there's been some community concern about where monetization might go with a new publisher involved; nothing is confirmed as of writing, but I'd keep an eye on it if that matters to you.

**Notes and concerns:**
- Early Access game: playable but unfinished, with more content planned over the next two or three years.
- PC and Xbox Series X/S only at launch; no PlayStation or Switch version yet.
- The price will increase after Early Access ends.
- Community has raised concerns about possible future monetization (battle pass style) under the new publisher; nothing confirmed as of writing.

## Total War: WARHAMMER (`total-war-warhammer`)

This one's for the strategy-minded kid who wants to command armies rather than be a soldier. You run a turn-based campaign (economy, diplomacy, recruitment) and then step into real-time battles where hundreds or thousands of units clash at once. What I love about it is that winning takes more than "bigger army wins": positioning, terrain, morale, and what your army is actually made of all matter, which is a genuinely good lesson in systems thinking. The Warhammer Fantasy setting means every faction plays completely differently, so there's no single right way to do things. I rate it 13+ because the violence is large-scale: battles can see thousands of enemies killed, though the base game keeps blood and gore fairly restrained (there is a paid DLC that adds considerably more). It's also a complex game, so younger players may need patience before it clicks.

**Notes and concerns:**
- Mass-casualty battles are the core of the game; individual deaths are abstract rather than graphic.
- A paid DLC pack adds significant blood and gore if you want to see it.
- Single-player campaigns have no chat; online multiplayer exists for those who use it.

## Total War: WARHAMMER II (`total-war-warhammer-2`)

If your child liked the first WARHAMMER, this one adds four more factions with genuinely different playstyles and a continent-spanning campaign built around a magical vortex that's slowly eating the world. The race-against-the-apocalypse framing is a nice touch; it gives long campaigns a real sense of urgency without changing how you actually play. Everything I said about the first game still applies: deep logistics, tactical battles where positioning beats raw numbers, and a fantasy setting with plenty to explore. I rate it 13+ for the same reasons as its predecessor (large-scale battle deaths, restrained gore in the base game). The new factions each bring genuinely different tactical identities, so there's plenty to compare and learn from even if you've played the first one. It's also where the trilogy starts feeling like a proper saga, which is part of why I keep it on the list. It's a big time commitment either way, so I'd only suggest it if your child already enjoys slow-burn strategy.

**Notes and concerns:**
- Same content profile as WARHAMMER I: mass casualties, limited blood in the base game.
- The same paid gore DLC is available for this title too.
- Campaigns are long; sessions can easily run hours at a time.

## Total War: WARHAMMER III (`total-war-warhammer-3`)

The third entry is where the trilogy really pays off, because it unlocks Immortal Empires, one giant combined campaign map with all three games' factions on it. You also get the Chaos Realms and some of the most visually striking content in the series; the demon-themed factions are a lot of fun to play even if they're not for everyone. The gameplay is the same deep Total War mix (turn-based strategy plus real-time battles), so this one makes the most sense as part two or three rather than a starting point. I rate it 13+ like the rest of the trilogy: thousands of units can die in a single battle, blood and gore are limited in the base game, and the paid DLC adds more if you want it. It's also a great conversation starter about how fantasy worlds get built; the lore here rewards players who actually read what they're playing. My students who've played it can talk your ear off about Chaos factions, and honestly that's a win. If your child is into big-picture strategy and doesn't mind long sessions, this is one of the most ambitious games on my list.

**Notes and concerns:**
- Best experienced with I and II installed; Immortal Empires combines all three campaigns.
- Same violence profile as the rest of the trilogy (mass casualties, restrained base-game gore).
- Chaos-themed content includes demons and darker fantasy imagery; still stylized rather than realistic.

## Mount & Blade: Warband (`mount-blade-warband`)

Warband is one of those rare games where you can basically do whatever you want: join a faction, start your own, trade goods between towns, or just ride around and see what happens. The battles are tactical rather than twitchy; you're placing units, using terrain, and making decisions under pressure, which is a great workout for planning skills. It's set in a fictional medieval world that borrows heavily from real history (knighthood, sieges, trade routes), so there's plenty to talk about after playing. I rate it 13+ because combat is constant and battles can see hundreds of soldiers killed, though the blood stays fairly restrained. There's no online chat required for any of it, which keeps things simple if your child is younger. And because so much of the game is reading menus, trade prices, and troop descriptions in English, it doubles as quiet vocabulary practice. My students who play it tend to come back with opinions about medieval life that surprise me.

**Notes and concerns:**
- Violence is frequent but stylized; limited blood and gore.
- The base game is single-player; community-made multiplayer servers exist if your child wants them.
- Very open-ended, so younger players may need a nudge on what to do first.

## Mount & Blade II: Bannerlord (`mount-blade-2-bannerlord`)

Bannerlord keeps everything good about Warband while adding deeper kingdom management: clan politics, marriage, succession, and a more detailed economy. The battles are bigger too, with more units on screen and more to coordinate, which rewards the kind of calm, big-picture thinking I'm always trying to teach in class. It's still an open sandbox at heart; you can be a merchant, a diplomat, or a warlord, and the game won't stop you mixing those roles. I rate it 13+ for the same reasons as Warband: lots of violence with limited blood, and battles where hundreds of soldiers die. The political layer is where it really shines; players end up negotiating, marrying into clans, and managing succession, which is a surprisingly good lesson in long-term consequences. It's also one of the better English listening experiences on this list, with plenty of character dialogue to soak up. If your child liked Warband, this is a natural next step.

**Notes and concerns:**
- Same content profile as Warband (frequent combat, restrained gore).
- Single-player focused; no built-in online chat in the base game.
- Deeper systems mean a steeper learning curve than its predecessor.

## Satisfactory (`satisfactory`)

Satisfactory is the best argument I have for why a game can teach real engineering thinking. You land on an alien planet and start by mining by hand, then gradually build conveyor-belt factories that process materials into increasingly complex products, and the satisfaction of watching a well-designed factory hum along is hard to overstate. The skills it builds are genuinely transferable: throughput, ratios, layout, and iterating when something breaks, which is basically how engineers actually work. My students who play it start using words like "bottleneck" and "throughput" in class without me asking. I rate it 13+ because the alien wildlife will attack you and you have to fight back; some of the larger creatures are also pretty scary. It's a co-op game too, so siblings or friends can build together.

**Notes and concerns:**
- Wildlife attacks are real; some creatures are designed to be intimidating.
- Official online co-op for up to four players.
- Extremely absorbing; "just one more upgrade" is a genuine time-sink in the best way.

## FTL: Faster Than Light (`ftl`)

FTL is a small game with an enormous amount of thinking packed into it. You command a lone starship fleeing a rebel fleet, and every jump brings a new decision: where do I put my limited power, which weapons do I fire, who covers the engine room while we're under attack? It's real-time with pause, so you can think as things happen rather than needing fast reflexes. Runs are short enough that failure feels like information instead of punishment, which is exactly how I want my students to treat mistakes in class. The violence is minimal (spaceship combat and the occasional crew casualty), which is why I rate it 10+ with a low violence tag. If your child likes puzzles but wants them to feel urgent, this is the one.

**Notes and concerns:**
- Crew members can die permanently; some players find that frustrating rather than disturbing.
- No online component at all; fully offline single-player.
- Roguelike structure means you will lose a lot before you win; patience required.

## Rocket League (`rocket-league`)

Rocket League is soccer played with rocket-powered cars, and it's one of the best examples I know of a game that looks simple and hides real depth underneath. The controls take five minutes to learn and years to master, which keeps players engaged without any pressure from difficulty spikes. It's also quietly a great language game: teams communicate constantly in short phrases ("nice save", "on your left", "go go go"), so my students pick up practical spoken English just by playing with others. There's no violence at all; the closest thing to conflict is a car flipping another car into the stands. The good news is that chat can be limited or turned off entirely on most platforms if you'd rather keep it clean. And because it's free to play, there's no pressure to buy anything before your child decides they even like it. My one honest caveat is that online matches put players in contact with strangers, and some of them talk badly.

**Notes and concerns:**
- Open chat with other online players is the main content concern; it can be limited or turned off on most platforms.
- Cosmetic items (car skins, trails) can be bought with real money, but nothing affects gameplay.
- No violence, no blood, no scary elements.

## Hades (`hades`)

Hades has the best writing I've seen in any game on this list. You play Zagreus, son of Hades, trying to escape the Underworld, and every time you fail (and you will) you get to talk with the cast of Greek gods back at home, which is where the real charm lives. The combat rewards building synergies between weapons and god-blessings, so each run becomes a little experiment in what works together; that's systems thinking disguised as action. For my students it's also one of the best listening experiences available: fast, witty, natural English dialogue with plenty of mythology vocabulary to pick up along the way. It's also one of the best value-for-money games on this list; there's no microtransaction or online component anywhere. If your child likes action games but you want something with actual writing behind it, start here. I rate it 10+ because there's a lot of cartoonish beat-em-up violence, and the dialogue does include occasional mild swearing, references to wine, and a couple of suggestive moments that are implied rather than shown.

**Notes and concerns:**
- Occasional profanity in dialogue (mild; one word stands out more than others).
- Alcohol references throughout (Dionysus is the god of wine, after all) and a few suggestive themes.
- No online component; fully single-player.

## Hades II (`hades-2`)

Hades II follows Melinoë, a witch-in-training taking the fight to the Titan Chronos, and it keeps everything that made the first one special while adding more depth to the build-crafting systems. The cast is bigger and the relationships between characters are even better written, so the "talk with the gods between runs" loop that I love about this series gets stronger rather than weaker. It's still a roguelike at heart: fail often, learn fast, try again smarter. I rate it 10+ for the same reasons as Hades (cartoonish combat violence plus mild language and suggestive themes in the dialogue). The new cast brings fresh mythology to explore, and the dialogue keeps that same sharp, natural English my students pick up so easily from the first one. It's also completely single-player with no microtransactions, which makes it a low-drama purchase. If your child liked the first game, this one is an easy recommendation.

**Notes and concerns:**
- Same content profile as Hades: cartoonish violence, occasional mild swearing, wine references.
- No online component; fully single-player.

## Battlefleet Gothic: Armada 2 (`battlefleet-gothic-2`)

Battlefleet Gothic is the gentler door into the Warhammer universe if your child likes strategy but not yet the full Total War commitment. You command capital ships in real-time fleet battles, and it's all about positioning, firing arcs, and timing rather than raw numbers; a well-placed ship beats three badly placed ones every time. The campaign spans several very different factions, so there's plenty to compare and learn from. I rate it 13+ because the situations get tense and ships (and crews) are lost in large numbers, even if nothing on screen is graphic. The campaign is single-player focused, so there's no online chat to worry about unless your child chooses multiplayer. It's also a good fit for kids who like reading and planning; the interface is full of useful military vocabulary (firing arcs, broadside, maneuvering) that they'll pick up without being quizzed on it. It pairs nicely with a Total War game later on as a step up in scale.

**Notes and concerns:**
- Space combat only; no blood or gore to speak of.
- Tense, high-stakes moments when your flagship is under fire.
- Single-player focused; online multiplayer available if used.

## Sid Meier's Civilization VI (`civilization-6`)

Civilization is the game I point to whenever someone asks me what a video game can actually teach. You guide one civilization from the ancient era all the way to the modern age, and every turn forces trade-offs: do we research this technology or build that wonder, do we trade with our neighbor or prepare for war? It's a masterclass in long-term thinking, because decisions you make early quietly shape everything that comes after. The historical setting means players pick up real vocabulary (diplomacy, espionage, infrastructure) and start asking questions about why civilizations rise and fall. I rate it 10+ because soldiers fight and die on the battlefield, though they just fall over and nothing is graphic at all. If your child can sit with a slow game, this one will reward them for years.

**Notes and concerns:**
- Battlefield deaths are abstract (units fall over); no blood or gore.
- Online multiplayer exists; single-player campaigns don't need it.
- "One more turn" is a real thing; sessions can run long.

## Blood Bowl 2 (`blood-bowl-2`)

Blood Bowl is American football played with fantasy races, where the tackles are lethal and the dice are cruel, and I mean that as a compliment. It's turn-based tactics at its finest: you're managing risk on every single play, deciding whether to gamble on a big gain or protect your star player, which teaches probability thinking better than any worksheet ever has. Team composition matters as much as in-game decisions; some rosters are built for speed, others for brute force, and both can win if played well. I rate it 10+ because players brawl on the pitch and there's some blood splattering, though it stays limited and cartoonish. The fantasy setting keeps the tone comedic even when things get rough; think cartoon violence rather than anything realistic. It's also a nice vocabulary builder, with plenty of sports and strategy terms used in natural context. It's a great game to play against a friend or sibling too, since matches are short enough for a proper duel after dinner.

**Notes and concerns:**
- Some blood splatter during big hits; stylized rather than graphic.
- The fantasy setting includes goblins, orcs, and other "rough" races; tone is comedic.
- Online multiplayer available if used.

## Blood Bowl 3 (`blood-bowl-3`)

Blood Bowl 3 keeps the same brutal turn-based football formula with an expanded set of factions and a formation editor that makes match setup faster than before. The core skill it builds hasn't changed: reading probability, managing risk on every play, and committing to a team identity rather than reacting to whatever happens. I'll be straight with you though; this one has been received more mixedly than Blood Bowl 2, and some players still prefer the older title's feel. If your child already loves Blood Bowl 2, they may want to try both before deciding which one is "theirs." The content profile is the same as its predecessor: pitch brawls with limited blood splatter, all in a comedic fantasy setting. It's also a nice one for teaching sports vocabulary in context; terms like "block", "tackle", and "rally" come up constantly during play. It's also a solid probability lesson in disguise; my students who play it get surprisingly good at thinking in odds and expected value. I rate it 10+ for those reasons and nothing more.

**Notes and concerns:**
- Same content profile as Blood Bowl 2 (stylized violence, limited blood).
- Reception has been mixed compared to Blood Bowl 2; some players prefer the older game.
- Online multiplayer available if used.

## Halo: The Master Chief Collection (`halo-mcc`)

Halo is where a generation of players learned what a great shooter campaign feels like, and this collection bundles all the mainline stories together in remastered form. The campaigns are strong storytelling with memorable characters, and the classic multiplayer maps shaped an entire genre; both are worth your child's time if they're into action games. I rate it 13+ because there's a lot of shooting: enemies break apart when shot, blood splatters onto the map in most titles, and some of the alien enemies are genuinely scary. It's also a collection with moving parts, so it's worth knowing what you're actually buying before you do it. The campaigns are also great English listening practice; the dialogue is natural and context-rich, which my students benefit from more than they'd expect. If you're buying it for a younger player who's still building up to 13+, I'd start with the earlier Halo titles in the collection rather than jumping straight to the later ones. If your child is old enough for the violence, this is one of the best value-for-money libraries in gaming.

**Notes and concerns:**
- Frequent shooting; some enemies disintegrate when killed and blood splatters on surfaces.
- Some enemies (especially the later ones) are designed to be frightening.
- The collection includes paid DLC campaigns and real-money cosmetics for multiplayer; base game is complete without them.
- Online multiplayer puts players in contact with strangers, including voice chat if enabled.

## The Long Dark (`the-long-dark`)

The Long Dark is survival without the fantasy: a geomagnetic disaster has knocked out the world, and you're alone in the Canadian wilderness trying to stay alive. There's no story to chase and no villain to defeat; the enemy is cold, hunger, fatigue, and your own bad decisions, which makes it one of the most honest planning games I know. Every action costs energy, so players learn to budget their effort the way you'd budget money. I rate it 10+ because animals will hunt you and you'll have to kill them for food (and self-defense), frozen bodies can be found in the wild, and some moments are genuinely tense or scary. There's no online chat in the core experience, which keeps it a quiet, focused play for most families. It's also one of the better survival-vocabulary builders on this list; words like "calories", "insulation", and "shelter" get used constantly and in context. It's a slow game, and that slowness is exactly the point.

**Notes and concerns:**
- Hunting and killing animals is part of survival; some encounters with predators are frightening.
- Frozen human bodies appear in the world; handled matter-of-factly rather than graphically.
- Official co-op mode is available if your child wants to play with a friend or sibling.

## Balatro (`balatro`)

Balatro looks like a card game and plays like a chess match wearing a poker mask. The rules are simple enough to explain in two minutes (make poker hands, score points, jokers modify everything), but the strategy underneath is deep enough that my strongest students still get beaten by it. It's pure logic and pattern recognition: you're constantly calculating odds, spotting combos, and deciding which upgrade path fits the hand you've been dealt. There's no violence at all, no online component, and sessions are short, which makes it perfect for a break between homework blocks. I rate it 10+ simply because of how absorbing it is; "one more round" can quietly become two hours. If your child likes math but thinks they hate it, this might change their mind.

**Notes and concerns:**
- Nothing to report content-wise; no violence, no chat, no online component.
- Poker-themed scoring only; there's no gambling or real money involved anywhere.
- Extremely replayable and absorbing; worth setting a time limit if your child is prone to losing track of it.

## Valheim (`valheim`)

Valheim drops you into a Viking afterlife where the way forward is, frankly, to die a boss and try again harder. The loop (explore a biome, gather resources, build a base, challenge the next boss) is one of the cleanest progression systems in gaming, and it teaches resource planning without ever feeling like homework. Base building here has real engineering weight to it: foundations have to sit on level ground, walls need support, and a badly planned longhouse will collapse on you. It's also one of the best co-op games on this list, so siblings or friends can build together online. The co-op mode is with friends only in practice, so there's no open chat with strangers to worry about. It's also a great game for teaching patience; failing a boss and trying again smarter is basically the whole lesson, and my students transfer that mindset straight into class. I rate it 10+ because combat against enemies and bosses is frequent, and some of the creatures (and the atmosphere) can be scary or tense.

**Notes and concerns:**
- Frequent combat; boss fights are intense and can be frightening for younger players.
- Online co-op with friends; no open chat with strangers in normal play.
- Viking setting includes skeletons and a few grim touches, all stylized.

## Minecraft (`minecraft`)

I'll be honest: I don't need to sell Minecraft, but I will tell you what it does that nothing else on this list quite matches. Open-ended building is one of the best creativity outlets in gaming, period; my students who build in Minecraft are doing spatial reasoning, planning, and problem-solving every single time they place a block. Redstone circuits are genuine logic gates, and kids who "just play" with them are learning how computers actually think without realizing it. The survival mode adds resource management on top of all that. If you keep them on a private server or in single-player, the content surface stays small and easy to understand. There's also a genuine social side if your child wants it; building together with a sibling or friend is half the fun. I rate it 10+ because the violence is mild and bloodless (monsters get punched), some nighttime moments can be tense or scary, and if your child plays online servers or mods, the content there is whatever other people have made, so it's worth knowing what they're joining.

**Notes and concerns:**
- Mild, bloodless violence; monsters are cartoonish.
- Some elements (caves, certain mobs) can be scary for younger players.
- Online servers, marketplace content, and mods may contain inappropriate material or more violence; this is the one to supervise if your child plays with others.

## Cities: Skylines (`cities-skylines`)

Cities: Skylines is urban planning as a game, and it's remarkably good at making you feel what city planners actually deal with. You zone land, wire up utilities, route roads, and balance a budget, then watch your decisions play out in traffic jams, housing prices, and the slow growth of a real-feeling town. The trade-offs are genuinely hard: fix the highway and you've just created a pollution corridor, solve the water pressure and now the budget is crying. My students who play it start asking me why our own city does things the way it does, which I take as a win. There's no violence at all; the closest thing to conflict is a bus that won't stop. I rate it 10+ because residents do die (accidents, illness) and you have to manage that, though nothing on screen is graphic.

**Notes and concerns:**
- No violence or blood; deaths are handled as statistics rather than events.
- No online component in the base game experience.
- Very absorbing for players who like systems; "one more district" runs long.

## Cities: Skylines II (`cities-skylines-2`)

Cities: Skylines II is the same city-building core with a deeper simulation underneath: economy, services, and infrastructure all have more moving parts this time around, which means your cities feel more alive and your mistakes hurt more. The reward for careful planning is bigger too; a well-designed district in this one really does hum. I'd suggest it to the same kids I'd suggest the first game to, with one caveat: it's a heavier simulation, so younger players may find the systems a little dense at first. There's no violence at all, and the content profile is identical to its predecessor (residents die as part of normal city life, handled as statistics). The deeper simulation also means more vocabulary in context; budgeting, zoning, infrastructure, and services all get used naturally as players talk through their plans. It's a quiet game with no online pressure, which makes it easy to fit into an evening without drama. If your child already loves building cities, this is the natural next step; if they're new to the genre, either entry works as a starting point.

**Notes and concerns:**
- No violence or blood; deaths are abstracted into management stats.
- Steeper learning curve than Cities: Skylines due to deeper systems.
- No online component in the base game experience.

## Company of Heroes (`company-of-heroes`)

Company of Heroes is World War II told through squad tactics, and it's one of the sharpest strategy games ever made. Cover, terrain, and positioning decide every fight; you're not out-gunning the enemy, you're out-thinking them with a handful of soldiers who can be permanently lost. That weight makes every decision matter in a way most strategy games never achieve. I rate it 16+, and I'm not being cautious for no reason: there's lots of violence and blood, some dismemberment, and adult language throughout the radio chatter. If your child is ready for that level of content and has an interest in WWII history (or just good tactics), this is a genuinely great game. If they're younger than that, I'd wait; the age suggestion on my site exists for a reason.

**Notes and concerns:**
- Frequent graphic violence: blood, some dismemberment, soldiers killed in realistic combat.
- Adult language and profanity throughout (radio calls, unit chatter).
- Historical setting is real war; some players find the subject matter heavy even where the graphics are stylized.

## Company of Heroes 2 (`company-of-heroes-2`)

Company of Heroes 2 moves the same sharp squad tactics to the Eastern Front and adds dynamic weather (snow, fog, rain) that genuinely changes how you fight, which is a brilliant design choice because it forces players to adapt rather than memorize. Everything else from the first game carries over: cover-based combat, small squads with real roles, and battles where positioning beats brute force every time. I rate it 16+ for exactly the same reasons as its predecessor: lots of violence and blood, some dismemberment, and adult language throughout. The weather system also teaches adaptability in a way most strategy games don't; the same tactic that wins in clear skies can lose badly in a snowstorm. It's a single-player focused experience for the campaign, so there's no online chat to manage unless your child chooses multiplayer. If your child handled the first one well and wants more, this is the one to get; if they haven't played Company of Heroes yet, either entry works as a starting point.

**Notes and concerns:**
- Frequent graphic violence: blood, some dismemberment, realistic combat deaths.
- Adult language and profanity throughout.
- Eastern Front setting includes harsh winter conditions that are part of the gameplay pressure.

## Diplomacy is Not an Option (`diplomacy-is-not-an-option`)

Diplomacy is Not an Option is a tower-defense game with a satirical edge, where you defend a medieval settlement against endless waves of invaders while juggling the absurd bureaucracy of running a kingdom. The tone keeps things light even as the pressure climbs, which makes it a good fit for younger strategy players who might find darker games off-putting. The core skill is planning under escalating constraints: where do I put this wall, what do I upgrade first, and how do I survive the next wave when everything gets harder? It's a nice bridge game between pure tower defense and real RTS thinking. The satirical tone is also a nice hook for conversation; my students enjoy pointing out the game's jokes about bureaucracy and war long after they've finished playing. It's single-player focused, so there's no online chat or stranger exposure to think about. I rate it 10+ because many, many enemies are killed defending your settlement, though it's all presented in a stylized cartoon way with no blood to speak of.

**Notes and concerns:**
- Large numbers of enemy deaths; entirely stylized, no graphic violence.
- Dark humor about bureaucracy and war; the tone is comedic throughout.
- No online component; single-player focused.

## PC Building Simulator (`pc-building-simulator`)

PC Building Simulator is the rare game where the "educational" claim isn't a stretch at all: you assemble, upgrade, and repair real PCs using licensed hardware components, and the knowledge transfers directly to actual computer literacy. ## Warhammer 40,000: Dawn of War (`dawn-of-war`)

## Timberborn (`timberborn`)

## Besiege (`besiege`)

You play civilians trying to survive a city under siege, not soldiers, and the game forces genuinely difficult moral trade-offs: do we scavenge tonight (risking death) or stay safe (and risk starvation), do we help the stranger at our door (and feed one less of our own)? It's a rare war game that puts you on the side most games ignore, and it does so with real emotional weight. I rate it 13+ because of mature themes throughout: strong language, references to alcohol and tobacco, and content dealing with suicide, depression, and the psychological toll of siege life; the ESRB rates it M (17+) in North America for those reasons. It's also one of the better moral-reasoning exercises on this list; my students who play it come back with opinions about trade-offs that surprise me. And because there's no online component, the content surface stays small and easy to understand even though the themes are heavy. If your child is older, emotionally ready, and interested in what war actually does to ordinary people, this is a powerful experience; if they're younger or more sensitive, I'd wait.

**Notes and concerns:**
- Mature themes: suicide references, depression, starvation, moral dilemmas with no clean answers.
- Strong language, alcohol and tobacco use depicted.
- ESRB rated M (17+) in North America; my 13+ rating reflects the absence of graphic violence rather than a judgment on the themes.
- Best approached as a discussion starter afterward rather than just a game to play through.

## Frostpunk (`frostpunk`)

Frostpunk asks a question most city-builders never bother to ask: how far would you go to keep your people alive? You manage a frozen apocalypse city around a single generator, and the laws you pass (child labor, faith, rationing) carry genuine moral weight because they have real consequences for specific characters on screen. It's city-building under existential pressure rather than optimization, which makes every decision feel heavier than in any other game on this list. I rate it 13+ because of mature themes throughout: characters die (including children, in some scenarios), the child labor law is a real mechanic you can choose to pass, and the overall tone is one of escalating moral compromise. It's not a violent game in the traditional sense; the violence is systemic rather than physical, which makes it arguably more disturbing for thoughtful players. If your child likes strategy games with real stakes and can handle morally complex content, this is a must-play.

**Notes and concerns:**
- Character deaths occur on screen, including children in some scenarios.
- The "Child Labor" law is a playable mechanic; passing it has visible consequences for child characters.
- Mature themes: moral compromise, societal collapse, difficult leadership decisions.
- No graphic violence or blood; the weight comes from choices rather than combat.

## Teardown (`teardown`)

Teardown is a voxel-based sandbox where every single building is fully destructible, and heist-style missions reward creative demolition as much as planning: how do I reach that vault without triggering the alarm, and what's the most efficient way to tear down three walls in the process? The physics are real (things fall, things break, momentum matters), so solutions emerge from experimentation rather than memorization, which is exactly the kind of out-of-the-box thinking I want my students practicing. It's also genuinely funny to watch your carefully planned route collapse into a pile of rubble, and the solutions players come up with are often smarter than anything the developers planned. It's a great spatial-reasoning workout too; planning a route through fully destructible buildings is basically puzzle-solving with a sledgehammer. The voxel style also keeps things light rather than violent-looking, which makes it an approachable pick even for younger strategy fans. I rate it 10+ because there's mischief and property destruction at its core (that's the whole game), plus optional PVP violence in multiplayer mods if your child chooses to use them; the base single-player experience has no blood or gore.

**Notes and concerns:**
- Property destruction is the core mechanic; stylized voxel graphics keep it light rather than violent-looking.
- Multiplayer mods add PvP with some violence; base game is single-player focused.
- No online chat required for the core experience.

## shapez 2: Factory (`shapez-2`)

shapez 2 is pure automation and logistics puzzle-solving, belt by belt: you cut, rotate, stack, and combine shapes on conveyor lines to satisfy increasingly complex orders, and the escalation in complexity teaches real systems thinking without any narrative dressing getting in the way. Multiple valid solutions are rewarded rather than punished, so creative approaches are as valued as efficient ones; that's exactly how I want my students approaching problems in class. It's also one of the cleaner games on this list content-wise: no violence, no chat, no online pressure, just you and a factory floor that wants to make sense. I rate it 10+ because it's a systems-heavy game that rewards patience and careful planning; there's nothing scary or violent about it at all. The difficulty also ramps up gradually, which means younger players can grow into it without feeling lost. If your child likes Satisfactory or Infinifactory but wants something more focused on pure logic, this is the one.

**Notes and concerns:**
- Nothing to report content-wise; completely clean.
- No online component required; single-player focused.
- The later levels are genuinely difficult; younger players may want a patient adult nearby for a hint now and then.

## Forza Horizon 5 (`forza-horizon-5`)

Forza Horizon 5 is open-world racing set in a vividly varied take on Mexico, and it's one of the most approachable racing games available: the handling is arcade-smooth enough for younger players, but there's real depth underneath for anyone who wants to chase lap times. The diverse biomes (desert, jungle, volcano) keep every race fresh, and the exploration reward structure means you don't have to be fast to enjoy it; discovering a hidden road through the jungle is its own kind of win. There's no violence at all; the closest thing to conflict is a car flipping another car into a cactus. I rate it 10+ because there's nothing to flag content-wise, and the online multiplayer (if used) puts players in contact with other racers but not with open chat by default. It's also one of the safer picks on this list for younger players who like driving games; there's no combat, no enemies, and no pressure to be fast. If your child likes driving games or just likes exploring big worlds, this is a safe and genuinely fun pick.

**Notes and concerns:**
- No violence, no blood, no scary elements of any kind.
- Online multiplayer available; cosmetic car purchases exist but nothing affects gameplay balance.
- Some cars are expensive in real life; the game doesn't promote or reference their cost.

## Forza Horizon 6 (`forza-horizon-6`)

Forza Horizon 6 takes the open-world racing festival to Japan, and it's the biggest map in the series so far: a stylized Tokyo as the hub city, rural roads through mountains and coast, and over 550 cars at launch. The handling is the same approachable arcade style as Forza Horizon 5, with real depth for players who want it, and the exploration reward structure means you don't have to be fast to enjoy it; discovering a scenic route through the countryside is its own kind of win. I rate it 10+ because there's no violence at all; the only content note worth mentioning is that mild profanity may appear in the soundtrack (some of the licensed music contains strong language). The online multiplayer, if used, puts players in contact with other racers but not with open chat by default. It's also one of the safer picks on this list for younger players who like driving games; there's no combat, no enemies, and no pressure to be fast. If your child liked Forza Horizon 5, this is an easy upgrade; if they're new to the series, either entry works as a starting point.

**Notes and concerns:**
- No violence of any kind.
- Mild profanity may be present in some licensed soundtrack tracks.
- Online multiplayer available; cosmetic car purchases exist but nothing affects gameplay balance.

## Descenders (`descenders`)

Descenders is momentum-based downhill mountain biking on procedurally generated tracks, and it's one of the most satisfying "feel good" games on this list: the physics reward smooth, deliberate riding over twitchy inputs, which teaches the same kind of body-awareness your child gets from any sport. The roguelite run structure (bomb down a mountain, collect parts, upgrade your bike, try again) keeps sessions short and replayable without ever feeling repetitive. There's no violence at all; the closest thing to conflict is a rock that sends you tumbling into a bush. I rate it 10+ because there's nothing to flag content-wise, and the procedural generation means every run is different enough to stay fresh. It's also one of the calmer games on this list; no chat, no online pressure, just you and a mountain that wants to be beaten. If your child likes rhythm games or sports games but wants something with more exploration, this is a great fit.

**Notes and concerns:**
- No violence, no blood, no scary elements of any kind.
- No online component required; single-player focused with some multiplayer options.
- The "bombing" phase (planning your line before you drop) rewards the same spatial thinking as any strategy game.
